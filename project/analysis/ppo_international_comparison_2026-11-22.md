# Public Policy Office: Comparison with Best Practice (2026-11-22)

**Purpose:** at the custodian's request ("compare our PPO to best practices of comparable real world institutions"), this memo compares the [Public Policy Office draft](../plans/public_policy_office_draft_2026-11-21.md) (Subcore 7-[P]) with institutions that evaluate proposed policy elsewhere, and proposes changes. Companion memo: the [Public Stewardship Office comparison](pso_international_comparison_2026-11-22.md).

**How this memo was made:** it summarizes well-known features of each institution from memory, and points to sources located by web search on 2026-11-22. The sources were identified, not read in full. Confirm any figure or date against its source before citing it. The memo is non-operative: it changes no draft text.

## Summary

The draft matches or exceeds best practice on independence, openness, review after adoption, and who can ask for an evaluation. It falls short of established practice in three places:

- **Access to information:** it has no right to the government's own data and analysis, which the OECD treats as essential for bodies like this.
- **Late changes:** it has no rule for re-evaluating a proposal that is amended after its evaluation.
- **Sponsor analysis:** it does all the analysis itself rather than also checking the sponsor's own impact analysis.

Six recommendations are at the end.

## 1. The Comparators

### 1.1 Independent cost and effect estimators

- **United States, Congressional Budget Office (1974).**
    - Estimates the cost of nearly every bill reported by a committee, and of major amendments as a bill moves.
    - Publishes long-term projections and reviews its own past accuracy.
    - Makes no policy recommendations, and has begun releasing some of its models publicly.
- **Netherlands, CPB Netherlands Bureau for Economic Policy Analysis (1945).**
    - The oldest body of this kind.
    - Before each election, parties may voluntarily submit their manifestos, and the CPB estimates their effects on the same models for every party ("Keuzes in Kaart").
- **Australia, Parliamentary Budget Office (2012).**
    - Any member of parliament may request a costing.
    - Requests may be kept confidential until the requester releases them.
    - After each general election it publishes a report costing every major party's commitments on the same basis.
- **United Kingdom, Office for Budget Responsibility (2010).**
    - Certifies the government's costings of budget measures.
    - Publishes a yearly evaluation of its own forecasts.
    - Has statutory access to government information.
- **Canada, Parliamentary Budget Officer:** costs proposals on request from parliamentarians, and has a statutory right of access to departmental data.
- **The OECD Principles for Independent Fiscal Institutions (2014):** set the common standard for bodies of this kind:
    - leadership chosen on merit and protected from removal for its findings;
    - funding protected over several years;
    - full and timely access to government information, set in law;
    - transparency of methods; and
    - regular external evaluation of the body's own work.

### 1.2 Reviewers of the government's own impact analysis

- **European Union, Regulatory Scrutiny Board.**
    - Reviews the Commission's impact assessments before a proposal goes forward.
    - A negative opinion sends the assessment back for revision. After a second negative opinion, the proposal can proceed only by an explicit political decision.
    - Its opinions are published, and in 2026 it rejected the impact assessment for a major tobacco proposal.
- **United Kingdom, Regulatory Policy Committee.**
    - Rates departments' impact assessments as fit or not fit for purpose (green, amber, red), and publishes every red rating.
    - Its reports track whether departments' analysis improves over time.
- **Germany, National Regulatory Control Council (Normenkontrollrat):** checks the compliance-cost estimates of every federal bill and publishes its opinion with the bill.
- **United States, Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs:**
    - reviews significant regulations against a published benefit-cost standard (Circular A-4);
    - sits inside the executive, so it is not independent.

### 1.3 Program evaluators and evidence bodies

- **United States, Washington State Institute for Public Policy:** publishes benefit-cost estimates of programs on one consistent model, used by the state legislature to choose among options.
- **United Kingdom, What Works Centres:** gather the evidence on what works in a policy area and publish it openly.
- **United Kingdom, post-implementation reviews:** many regulations must be reviewed after a set period, with the result published.
- **Mexico, CONEVAL (2005–2025):** an autonomous body that measured poverty and evaluated social programs, widely respected abroad. The 2024 constitutional reform eliminating autonomous bodies abolished it, and its functions passed to the national statistics office in 2025.
- **US Office of Technology Assessment:** abolished by statute in 1995.
- **Lesson from both:** how vulnerable such bodies are when ordinary law can end them.

### 1.4 Open policy models

- **OpenFisca:** open-source software, begun in France, that turns tax and benefit rules into code that anyone can run. It is used by public bodies in several countries.
- **PolicyEngine:** a civic project built on the same approach for the United States and the United Kingdom. It lets anyone model a tax or benefit change in a browser.
- **The EU's EUROMOD tax-benefit model:** made freely available for anyone to use.
- **Comparison with the draft:** most official estimators still publish their results and methods rather than runnable models. The draft's §9 goes further than common practice.

## 2. How the Draft Compares

| Feature | Best practice | How the draft compares |
|---|---|---|
| Independence | OECD principles; CBO, OBR | **Stronger.** Constitutional footing, seats drawn by lot, and protected funding answer the fate of CONEVAL and the Office of Technology Assessment |
| No recommendations, no ranking of aims | CBO, CPB | **Equal.** §8 bars ranking aims and advocacy |
| Coverage | CBO (bills); Regulatory Scrutiny Board and Regulatory Policy Committee (regulations) | **Broader.** Any binding policy above the materiality threshold, from any sponsor |
| Who can ask | Australian Parliamentary Budget Office and Canadian Parliamentary Budget Officer (any member of parliament) | **Broader.** Sponsors, the governing authority, petitions, forums, and anyone whose question uncovers a gap |
| Distributional analysis | Washington State Institute; tax-benefit models | **Equal.** §5 requires who gains and who bears costs, by group |
| Response duty | Regulatory Scrutiny Board (revise and resubmit, then political decision) | **Comparable.** Respond-or-explain on every material finding, without a veto |
| Review after adoption | UK post-implementation reviews | **Stronger.** Every evaluated policy gets a review date, five years at most by default |
| Accuracy reporting | OBR forecast evaluation; CBO record of its projections | **Equal.** Yearly accuracy report |
| Open models | OpenFisca, PolicyEngine, EUROMOD | **Stronger than official practice.** Runnable open models and reproducible evaluations |
| Emergency measures | Rarely covered | **Stronger.** Tied to the Chapter Twenty-Three continuation burden |
| Access to information | OECD principle; OBR and Canadian Parliamentary Budget Officer powers in law | **Missing.** No right to data, models, or explanations from public bodies |
| Amendments | CBO estimates major amendments | **Missing.** No rule for proposals changed after evaluation |
| Sponsor's own analysis | Regulatory Scrutiny Board, Regulatory Policy Committee, Normenkontrollrat | **Missing.** The office does all the analysis; sponsors need not show theirs |
| Election platform costing | CPB, Australian Parliamentary Budget Office | **Not covered.** |
| External evaluation of the office | OECD principle | **Partly.** Accuracy report and challenge route, but no periodic outside review |

## 3. Recommendations

1. **A right of access to information.** The office may obtain the data, models, analysis, and explanations it needs from any public body and from a proposal's sponsor, within a published time. A refusal or delay is published and may be challenged before a forum. Protected data stays under the standard data-type rules. This closes the main gap against the OECD principles.
2. **Re-evaluate material amendments.** When a proposal is amended after its evaluation in a way that materially changes its likely effects, the office publishes a short-form update before the final decision. The decision may not be taken until that update is out, or the time limit for it has passed.
3. **Ask the sponsor to show its work.** Above the threshold, a sponsor submits its own impact statement on the published template. The office rates it as fit or not fit for purpose, as the Regulatory Policy Committee does, and makes its own estimate where it is material or contested. This adds a check on the sponsor's reasoning, raises the quality of sponsor analysis over time, and frees office capacity.
4. **Offer equal platform costing before contested selections.** Any candidate or party may submit its main proposals for evaluation before a contested selection, on the same methods and timetable for all. Results are published together, never rank aims, and are never required. This follows the CPB and the Australian Parliamentary Budget Office.
5. **Allow drafting help, confidential only until introduction.** Any sponsor may ask for technical help while drafting. The help stays confidential only until the proposal is introduced or published, and then the record is published in full. The open models of §9 already let anyone do much of this themselves.
6. **External review of the office.** Every few years an outside panel reviews the office's methods, accuracy, and independence, and publishes its findings with the office's response.
