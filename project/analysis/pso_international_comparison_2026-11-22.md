# Public Stewardship Office: International Comparison (2026-11-22)

**Purpose:** at the custodian's request ("take our PSO draft and compare it to comparable institutions worldwide and see how we do"), this memo compares the [Public Stewardship Office draft](../plans/public_stewardship_office_draft_2026-11-21.md) (Subcore 7-[S]) with institutions that do similar work elsewhere, and proposes changes.

**How this memo was made:** it summarizes well-known features of each institution from memory, and points to sources located by web search on 2026-11-22. The sources were identified, not read in full. Confirm any figure or date against its source before citing it. The memo is non-operative: it changes no draft text.

## Summary

No single institution anywhere combines everything the draft does. Each part of the draft has a real-world model, so the design is not untested, but the combination is new. Three things stand out:

- **Strongest points:** independence and entrenchment, a response duty with deadlines, and the reach of required planning. Several real future-generations bodies were weakened or abolished because a statute could undo them (Israel, Hungary, the US Office of Technology Assessment). The draft's constitutional footing, terms drawn by lot, and protected funding answer that directly.
- **Main risks:** the scope is the broadest of any comparator, and the office would both rate plans and hand out money. Wales's experience shows that a duty covering everything tends to produce box-ticking. Every comparator that hands out money keeps that apart from the body that judges performance.
- **What to change:** five recommendations at the end, the most important being a firewall between the Stewardship Fund and the reviewing and rating work, and a phased start for private Class C bodies.

## 1. The Comparators

### 1.1 Future-generations guardians

- **Wales, Future Generations Commissioner** (Well-being of Future Generations (Wales) Act 2015). The closest single comparator.
    - About fifty named public bodies must set well-being objectives against seven national goals and take all reasonable steps to meet them.
    - The Commissioner advises them, reviews them (section 20 reviews), and makes recommendations, which a body must follow or explain why not.
    - Every five years, the year before the Senedd election, the Commissioner publishes a Future Generations Report; the Welsh Government responds in public, as it did to the 2025 report.
    - The Commissioner serves one seven-year term but is appointed by Welsh Ministers. The office has no enforcement power and covers no private bodies.
    - The Senedd's ten-year post-legislative scrutiny is examining whether the duties are too broad and too weakly enforced.
- **Hungary, Ombudsman for Future Generations.** Created in 2008 as a stand-alone parliamentary commissioner with power to investigate and intervene in proceedings. In 2012 it was folded into the Commissioner for Fundamental Rights as a deputy. That downgrade is often cited as an example of how a statute-based guardian can be weakened.
- **Israel, Knesset Commission for Future Generations (2001–2006).** It could request information and comment on bills affecting future generations. It was not renewed after its first term, which is the standard example of how such a body can simply lapse.
- **Finland, Committee for the Future** (parliamentary standing committee) and the Government Report on the Future once each electoral term. Finland also has **Sitra**, an independent future fund answerable to Parliament and paid for from an endowment, which pays for experiments and foresight. It is the nearest model for joining foresight and money.
- **United Nations, Declaration on Future Generations** (Summit of the Future, September 2024). It commits states to consider future generations and calls for a Special Envoy. Commentators describe its follow-through as slow.
- **European Union.** The Commission publishes an annual Strategic Foresight Report, and since 2024 one Commissioner's portfolio includes intergenerational fairness.

### 1.2 Independent advisers with a response duty

- **United Kingdom, Climate Change Committee** (Climate Change Act 2008).
    - It advises on five-year carbon budgets set well ahead and reports to Parliament on progress every year.
    - The government must publish a response by a deadline set in the Act, and it does so each year.
    - The government must also publish a plan to meet each budget. The courts found the government's delivery plan inadequate in 2022 and again in 2024, which shows that a plan's adequacy can be tested before a court.
    - The Committee grades how credible the policies are, a working model for a rating ladder.
- **New Zealand, long-term insights briefings** (Public Service Act 2020). Each department's chief executive must publish, at least once every three years, a briefing on long-term trends, risks, and opportunities. Departments write these independently of ministers and consult the public on the topic and the draft. Reviews find the quality uneven.
- **Fiscal outlooks:**
    - Australia's Intergenerational Report looks 40 years ahead, at least every five years, but the Treasury prepares it, so it is not independent.
    - The US Congressional Budget Office publishes a 30-year long-term budget outlook every year.
    - The UK Office for Budget Responsibility publishes long-term fiscal projections and a yearly evaluation of its own forecasts, a model for the draft's look-back.

### 1.3 Foresight units inside government

- **Singapore, Centre for Strategic Futures**, inside the Prime Minister's Office, runs scenario planning for government.
- **Japan, "Future Design"**, used in some municipalities: residents take the role of imaginary future residents in planning sessions, a model for the draft's future-generations voice.
- **Germany:** a parliamentary sustainability council reviews bills for sustainability. In 2021 the Federal Constitutional Court held that the climate law unfairly pushed the burden of cutting emissions onto later generations. That is the closest real-world precedent for treating intergenerational fairness as a constitutional duty that a court can enforce.

### 1.4 Required plans from private bodies

- **France, Duty of Vigilance law (2017).** Very large companies must publish and carry out a vigilance plan covering human-rights and environmental risks. Anyone with an interest can ask a court to order a company to comply. It is the closest model for the draft's Class C plans with a forum route.
- **European Union, sustainability reporting and due-diligence rules.** These require large companies to report on sustainability and, in some versions, to adopt climate transition plans. The 2025 simplification package narrowed both. This shows the political pressure that duties on private bodies attract.

### 1.5 Research and prize funding

- **United Kingdom, ARIA** (Advanced Research and Invention Agency, 2023): a multi-year budget protected from yearly cuts, programme managers with wide freedom, and a stated tolerance for failure. **US DARPA and ARPA-H** follow a similar model.
- **European Research Council:** an independent scientific council chooses grants on excellence alone, with Starting Grants reserved for early-career researchers. It is the model for the draft's set-aside for new teams.
- **US National Science Foundation:** EAGER grants for early-stage, high-risk ideas, a model for the unconventional-research set-aside.
- **Prizes and advance commitments:**
    - The 1714 Longitude Prize and its modern successor, the Longitude Prize on antibiotic resistance, awarded in 2024.
    - The pneumococcal vaccine Advance Market Commitment (2009), which paid for a vaccine for low-income countries.
    - XPRIZE.
    - Frontier, a commitment to buy carbon removal in advance.
- **Rating research results:** the UK Research Excellence Framework rates outputs on a star scale and uses the ratings to allocate funding. Australia's Excellence in Research for Australia used a five-point scale and was ended in 2023, partly because of its cost.

## 2. How the Draft Compares

| Feature | Closest comparator | How the draft compares |
|---|---|---|
| Independence | Wales (one 7-year term, ministerial appointment); CCC (statutory) | **Stronger.** Constitutional footing, seats drawn by lot, 8-year staggered terms, and protected funding answer the lapse of Israel's body and the downgrade of Hungary's |
| Horizon | CBO 30 years; Australia 40; Wales 25+ | **Longer.** Five horizons out to 100 years, with less detail further out, and a yearly look-back like the OBR's |
| Response duty | CCC (statutory deadline); Wales (follow or explain) | **Equal to the best.** The governing authority answers every guidance item by a fixed date |
| Who must plan | Wales (about 50 public bodies); NZ (all departments); France (largest companies) | **Broadest.** All public bodies and all Class C-or-higher systems and institutions, public or private |
| Planning cycle | CCC June report, October response; the EU's yearly cascade from Commission to member states | **Comparable.** The April, July, and October cascade follows the same pattern of advice, then response, then the next layer |
| Rating of plans | CCC's credibility grades; audit offices' examinations | **Stronger, if the rubric holds.** A published five-point ladder tied to a forum route is rare |
| Enforcement | UK and French courts testing plan adequacy | **Comparable.** A forum may order a revised plan but not its content, as those courts have done |
| Fund | Sitra (endowment); ARIA (protected multi-year budget) | **New combination.** No comparator joins a foresight office, an incentives budget, and a research fund |
| Research set-asides and result review | ERC Starting Grants; NSF EAGER; REF | **In line with practice.** Each piece has a working model |
| Advocacy | Wales Commissioner campaigns; CCC and OBR avoid advocacy | **Riskier.** Advocacy by the office that also rates plans can look partisan, even with the quiet period |

## 3. Risks the Comparators Reveal

1. **Too broad to bite.** Evidence to the Welsh post-legislative scrutiny describes objectives that cover everything and so steer nothing, and reports written to comply rather than to plan. The draft's scope is wider than Wales's. Without a way to focus, plans may become paperwork.
2. **One body as judge and paymaster.** The office both rates a body's plan and can fund that body through an incentive or grant. Comparators keep these apart: the CCC advises but funds nothing, and ARIA funds but rates no one's plans. The draft bars rewarding or punishing anyone for a plan's content (§8.6), but the risk of seeming to favour someone remains.
3. **Advocacy and neutrality.** Bodies trusted for neutral assessment, such as the CCC, the OBR, and the CBO, do not campaign. A rating from an office that also campaigns for outcomes is easier to dismiss as political.
4. **Burden on private bodies.** France's vigilance law covers only the very largest companies, and the EU narrowed its rules in 2025 after complaints about the burden. Class C reaches much further down. A yearly cycle for every Class C body at once may cost more than it gains in the first years.
5. **Cost of rating research.** Australia's research rating scheme ended partly because of its cost. A yearly five-point review of every grant, repeated after five years, needs a light process.
6. **Rubric drift.** A five-point ladder stays fair only if raters apply it the same way. Audit offices publish detailed criteria and calibrate their staff for this reason.

## 4. Recommendations

1. **Firewall the fund.** Run the Stewardship Fund through a separate fund board within the office, with its own seats drawn by lot. Staff who review and rate plans take no part in fund decisions, and the reverse.
2. **Separate advocacy from rating.** Keep the education and advocacy unit apart from the reviewers, and let the office advocate only for priorities stated in a published outlook, never for or against a specific body's plan.
3. **Focus the plans.** Require each plan to address the register risks the outlook marks as material for that kind of body, at most a small published number, plus any the body adds. This answers the main criticism of the Welsh model.
4. **Phase in private Class C bodies.** Start with public bodies and Class A and Class B in the first full cycle, add Class C in the next, and use short-form templates for Class C throughout.
5. **Publish and audit the rubric.** Publish detailed criteria and sample plans for each step of the alignment ladder, and have an outside auditor check a sample of ratings each year for consistency. Keep the research results review light: one short review at the end and a sampled second look at five years, rather than a full re-rating of every grant.
