# Trusted Voices in Surrender: Research Memo (2026-11-20)

**Purpose:** background for the *Trusted voices* rule added on 2026-11-20 to [Subcore 26-A §4.2 Before Force](../../subcore_26.010_special_enforcement_procedure.md#trusted-voices), with its core line in [Chapter Twenty-Six §5](../../core_26.000_special_enforcement.md#5-potential-violence-and-force). The custodian asked whether research supports calling in family and friends, voluntarily, to help persuade a violent sentient to surrender.

**How this memo was made:** it summarizes well-known findings from memory and points to sources located by web search on 2026-11-20. The sources were identified, not read in full; confirm any figure against the source before citing it.

## Summary

Research supports the idea, with conditions. Three bodies of work bear on it:

1. **Crisis and hostage negotiation** studies the exact case: family, friends, and clergy are called "third-party intermediaries" (TPIs). Police negotiators use them, and they can help bring a surrender, but unscreened face-to-face contact carries known risks.
2. **Focused deterrence** (Group Violence Intervention, Boston's Operation Ceasefire) uses family and community members as a "moral voice" against violence, and systematic reviews find a moderate reduction in violence.
3. **Fugitive Safe Surrender**, a US Marshals program, showed that many sentients wanted on warrants surrender peacefully when offered a trusted, non-police setting such as a church.

## 1. Third-party intermediaries in crisis negotiation

- **What helps:** a trusted voice can lower emotion, restore hope, and give the subject a reason to come out that does not feel like losing face. Negotiation syntheses list TPIs among the tools that can support peaceful resolution.
- **What goes wrong:** the literature warns that a subject may use an intermediary as an audience for suicide or violence, may take them hostage, or may be provoked by someone tied to the conflict (an estranged partner, for example). An intermediary may also say something unplanned that inflames the subject.
- **Common practice:** the negotiator interviews the intermediary first, decides whether they help or harm, coaches what to say, and keeps control of the channel: relayed messages, recorded messages, or phone contact with the negotiator present, rather than face-to-face meetings.
- **How the rule follows it:** screening and guidance by a trained negotiator; contact through the enforcement channel; face-to-face only where judged safe and never with an armed or hostage-holding sentient; no use as leverage or audience.

## 2. Focused deterrence and the community "moral voice"

- **What it is:** groups at highest risk of violence are called in. Law enforcement states the consequences of further violence; family, community members, clergy, and former offenders say the violence must stop and that they want the person alive and free; services are offered.
- **Evidence:** the Campbell Collaboration systematic review and meta-analysis (Braga, Weisburd, and Turchan) found that focused deterrence is associated with a moderate, statistically significant reduction in crime, strongest for gang and group violence.
- **Relevance:** it shows that a message from people the subject cares about carries weight that an order from an authority does not. It is a group, pre-crisis method, so it supports the principle more than the standoff tactic.

## 3. Fugitive Safe Surrender

- **What it is:** a US Marshals Service program, begun in Cleveland in 2005, in which sentients with outstanding warrants could surrender at a church or other community site over a few days, with clergy present.
- **Evidence:** Case Western Reserve University researchers evaluated it and reported that it works: large numbers surrendered peacefully, most of them for nonviolent offenses.
- **Relevance:** a trusted setting and trusted faces make surrender easier to choose, and remove the danger of an arrest by force.

Sources:
- *Offenders' safe surrender at churches works, according to CWRU research findings.* [case.edu](https://case.edu/news/offenders-safe-surrender-churches-works-according-cwru-research-findings)
- NCJRS entry on the program evaluation. [ojp.gov](https://ojp.gov/node/1278341)

## Limits of the evidence

- Most crisis-negotiation evidence comes from case reviews and practitioner reports, not controlled trials; it cannot isolate the effect of an intermediary from everything else in a standoff.
- Focused deterrence and safe surrender work before a crisis, with sentients who are not barricaded or attacking.
- No source found addresses non-biological sentients; the rule applies the same safeguards to them.
