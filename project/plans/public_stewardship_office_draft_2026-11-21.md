# SUBCORE 7-[S]: PUBLIC STEWARDSHIP OFFICE — LOOKING AHEAD FOR THOSE NOT YET HERE

### 1. Purpose, Role, and Scope

*In plain terms: those who govern are chosen for a few years at a time and are tempted to leave problems for later. An independent office looks decades ahead, says plainly what is coming, advises on what to do about it, and speaks up for the sentients who will live with today's choices.*

**Purpose:** Subcore 7-[S] makes sure that long-term risks, commitments, and opportunities are seen and weighed when decisions are made today, and that future generations have a voice when those decisions are made.

**Role:** Subcore 7-[S] is the constitutional owner of the **Long-Term Outlook**, **stewardship guidance**, **stewardship plans and progress reports**, the **Stewardship Fund**, and **future-generations submissions**. Where it sits:

- [Chapter Twenty-Seven §5 Strategy, Stewardship Direction, and Ecosystem Value](../../core_27.000_governance.md#5-strategy-stewardship-direction-and-ecosystem-value-non-corporate) puts the duty of stewardship on those who govern. This office informs that duty and holds public bodies to account for planning against it; it does not take it over.
- The Public Policy Office evaluates one proposal at a time. This office looks across all policy and over a longer horizon. For a proposal with long-horizon or irreversible effects, the two publish one combined evaluation.
- The Public Information Office produces the statistics and research this office relies on.
- The Public Debt Office and the Public Finance Office report the obligations that later generations will carry, and this office takes them into account.

**Scope:** The office:

- publishes the Long-Term Outlook (§4) and keeps the Long-Term Risk Register (§5);
- issues stewardship guidance and assesses the governing authority's response (§6);
- requires stewardship plans and yearly progress reports from public bodies and from every system or institution of Class C or higher, public or private, and reviews and publishes them (§7);
- runs the Stewardship Fund: incentives for technological, ecological, and social improvement, public education and advocacy on the long term, and grants for basic research (§8);
- prepares the long-horizon section of Public Policy Office evaluations (§9); and
- makes submissions for future generations (§10).

**Out of scope:** The office does not write any body's plan for it, allocate resources beyond its own Stewardship Fund, direct how any other body carries out its plan (the office runs its own plan and programs), impose penalties, require a Class L or Class P body to plan, or decide any matter that belongs to a forum.

### 2. Why the Public Stewardship Office Exists

*In plain terms: the future cannot vote, and the present is always tempted to borrow from it. Someone independent has to keep the long view in front of decision-makers.*

Those who govern answer to the sentients alive and able to vote now, over terms of a few years. Costs that fall decades away, slow-building risks, and harms that cannot be undone are easy to discount. Future generations, whom [Article I-C](../../core_04.000_rights_part_a.md#article-i-c-intergenerational-responsibility) (*Intergenerational Responsibility*) protects, have no one to speak for them when today's choices are made. An office that is independent of the political cycle, that looks ahead in public, and that may speak for the future without power to command it, keeps the long view in the room without taking the decision away from those who are accountable for it.

**Grounded in:**

### 3. Independence and Placement

*In plain terms: the office meets every test for an Independent Body, and its members stay longer than any one government so that no one government can bend it.*

The office is an Independent Body and meets [Chapter Seven §3 What Makes a Body Independent](../../core_07.000_independent_bodies.md#3-what-makes-a-body-independent) in full:

- **Decision seats:** drawn by lot from the candidates who pass the office's published competency gate, under the role rules of [Subcore 27-E §3 Role Criteria, Appointment Standards, and Removal Pathways](../../subcore_27.050_role_holders_interpretive_bodies.md#3-role-criteria-appointment-standards-and-removal-pathways).
- **Long, staggered terms:** members serve one term of eight years, not renewable, staggered so that no single selection cycle replaces a majority.
- **No removal for advice:** a member may be removed only for a published cause under Subcore 27-E §3. The content of the office's outlook, guidance, or submissions is never a cause.
- **Competencies:** the gate covers foresight and scenario methods, ecology, demography, public finance, and critical systems, and at least one seat is held by a sentient under the age the adopter sets for its youth seat.
- **Funding:** the office's running costs come through a floor set by the Public Finance Office, which may not be cut in response to the office's advice. The Stewardship Fund is a separate budget under §8.

### 4. Long-Term Outlook

*In plain terms: every April the office publishes a yearly report, and every fourth year that report is a full outlook: its best honest picture of where things are heading, five, ten, twenty-five, fifty, and a hundred years out, and what could go badly wrong or well. The nearer years get firm figures; the far years get broad scenarios.*

The office publishes a **Yearly Stewardship Report** on the first day of the planning cycle under §7.3 (by default, April 1). At least every fourth report, and the report before each regular contested selection where the cycle allows, is a full **Long-Term Outlook**. The reports in between update the outlook wherever the picture has changed. The outlook looks ahead over five horizons, with less detail the further out it looks:

- **5 years:** specific projections with figures, and the decisions that are due soon;
- **10 years:** projections with ranges, and the investments and commitments that take this long to bear fruit;
- **25 years:** the main trends and their likely ranges;
- **50 years:** a small set of plausible scenarios and what would tell them apart;
- **100 years:** broad scenarios, irreversible thresholds, and the conditions future generations are likely to inherit.

It looks further than 100 years where a risk requires it. Across these horizons, at a minimum the outlook covers:

- **Population and wellbeing:**
    - population size, age structure, and movement;
    - wellbeing, health, and survival-floor access; and
    - skills and education, and whether they match the work likely to be needed;
- **Technology:**
    - emerging technologies: what they could do, how soon they may be ready, what they may cost, and the risks they bring;
    - declining technologies: what is replacing them, which assets may be stranded, and which workers and communities will need help through the change;
- **Resources:**
    - resource uses that are rising, and how long supply can keep up;
    - resource uses that are falling, and what that frees up;
    - reserves and rates of depletion, substitutes, and the share that is reused or recycled;
- **Economy:**
    - cost trends for essentials such as energy, food, housing, key materials, and labour;
    - economic cycles: where the economy stands in the cycle, and the likely risk, depth, and timing of downturns;
    - shifts in work and employment, including work taken over by automation; and
    - the condition of [Productive Capacity](../../subcore_03.075_band_continuity.md#productive-capacity), including the ageing and replacement of infrastructure;
- **Ecology:** ecological limits and footprint, as Article I-B requires to be visible, including climate, biodiversity, soil, and water;
- **Critical systems:** the resilience of systems whose failure would be catastrophic or hard to recover from;
- **Obligations to the future:** public debt, unfunded commitments, maintenance left undone, and depleting resources that later generations will carry;
- **Opportunities:** developments that could markedly improve life, and what it would take to reach them;
- **Scenarios:** more than one plausible future, including severe ones, and what would tell them apart early; and
- **Confidence:** how sure the office is of each part, on the confidence ladder the Public Information Office uses.

### 5. Long-Term Risk Register

*In plain terms: a public list of the big long-term risks, kept up to date, so none of them can be quietly forgotten.*

- **The register:** the office keeps a public register of long-term risks and irreversible thresholds, with for each one its likelihood, its possible harm, how soon action would be needed, and who holds responsibility for it.
- **Updates:** the register is updated in each Yearly Stewardship Report, and at once whenever a material new risk is identified.
- **Escalation:** where a risk is near an irreversible threshold, the office publishes an alert and the governing authority must respond in public within a published time.

### 6. Stewardship Guidance and the Governing Authority's Response

*In plain terms: the office gives advice on priorities, and those who govern must say openly what they did with it.*

- **Guidance:** with each Yearly Stewardship Report, the office publishes or updates its stewardship guidance: the long-term priorities it judges most important and the options for meeting them. Guidance binds no one.
- **Response:** the governing authority's published strategy under [Chapter Twenty-Seven §5 Strategy, Stewardship Direction, and Ecosystem Value](../../core_27.000_governance.md#5-strategy-stewardship-direction-and-ecosystem-value-non-corporate) states, for each item of guidance, whether it adopts it, adapts it, or declines it, with reasons. The response is published with the public bodies' plans on the second date of the planning cycle under §7.3.
- **Progress assessment:** each Yearly Stewardship Report assesses how far the governing authority's strategy, and the plans and reports of the previous cycle, are meeting the long-term risks in the register.
- **Contestability:** a failure to publish a response is a procedural failure that may be challenged under [Chapter Twenty-Seven §3.1.4 Contestation, Correction, and Pluralism](../../core_27.000_governance.md#314-contestation-correction-and-pluralism). The merits of a response are for the public and the authorizing community to judge, not the office.

### 7. Stewardship Plans and Progress Reports

*In plain terms: those who run systems and institutions that matter to many sentients are already bound to act for those sentients. Acting for someone means planning for their future. So every public body, and every system or institution of Class C or higher, public or private, writes a plan that answers the outlook and reports on how it is doing. The biggest bodies do the most: Class A plans and reports in full every year, while Class C writes a short plan and reports every second year. Plans follow a yearly cascade: the office reports in April, public bodies plan in response by July, and everyone else plans in response to both by October. The office checks and publishes the plans and reports; it never writes them.*

Stewards already owe a duty to the sentients their system or institution affects under [Chapter Two §17.7 Duty to Represent Stakeholder Interests](../../core_02.040_stewardship_capacity_principles.md#177-duty-to-represent-stakeholder-interests). Representing someone's interests includes planning for their future, and planning serves both [Two Constitutional Aims](../../core_00.000_preamble.md#two-constitutional-aims): [Continuity](../../core_00.000_preamble.md#continuity), because risks seen and planned for early can be survived, and [Flourishing](../../core_00.000_preamble.md#flourishing), because wellbeing over time depends on choices made well ahead. The duty to plan therefore follows the size of a body's effect on sentients, not whether it is public or private. Those who hold authority keep the choice of what to do.

The subsections below say who must plan (§7.1), what a plan contains (§7.2), when plans are due (§7.3), how the office reviews them and where its power stops (§7.4), how each plan is rated and what a low rating opens to challenge (§7.5), what happens when a plan is missing (§7.6), and how planning appears in each System Charter and Institutional Charter (§7.7).

#### 7.1 Who Must Plan

The duty to plan falls on every public body and on every system or institution large enough to be Class C or higher.

- **Who must submit a stewardship plan:**
    - **Public bodies:**
        - the governing authority and each public office, department, or agency acting under it;
        - each Independent Body, for the risks in its own domain; and
        - any other public body that the outlook names as holding responsibility for a risk in the register;
    - **Systems and institutions:** every system or institution of Class A, Class B, or Class C under [Subcore 8-A §7 System Impact Classifications](../../subcore_08.010_system_classification.md#7-system-impact-classifications) or its institutional counterpart, whether public or private.
- **Below Class C:** a Class L or Class P system or institution may plan against the outlook but is not required to.

#### 7.2 What a Stewardship Plan Contains

Each stewardship plan answers the outlook for the body's own area and the sentients it affects. So that a plan steers rather than lists everything, each one focuses on a small number of material risks. It sets out:

- **the focus risks:** the register risks and priorities that the outlook marks as material for bodies of its kind and class, at most a published number (by default, five), together with any others the body chooses to add;
- what the body will do about each, by when, and with what resources, or why it will not act, in concrete steps for the 5- and 10-year horizons and as a stated direction for the 25-, 50-, and 100-year horizons;
- any other planning the body judges important to the sentients it represents;
- how the stakeholders it represents were heard in making the plan, through the routes [Article XII-A](../../core_04.010_rights_part_b.md#article-xii-a-stakeholder-system-participation-and-representation) (*Stakeholder System Participation and Representation*) requires;
- the indicators by which progress will be judged; and
- for a public body, how the plan fits the governing authority's strategy under [Chapter Twenty-Seven §5 Strategy, Stewardship Direction, and Ecosystem Value](../../core_27.000_governance.md#5-strategy-stewardship-direction-and-ecosystem-value-non-corporate).

#### 7.3 The Yearly Planning Cycle

Plans follow a yearly cascade, so that each layer can respond to the one before it and new information spreads through planning smoothly instead of all at once.

- **The three dates:**
    - **April 1:** the office publishes its Yearly Stewardship Report under §4, including its review of the previous cycle's plans and reports;
    - **July 1:** the governing authority, its offices, departments, and agencies, each Independent Body, and every other public body that must plan publish their plans and progress reports in response, and the governing authority publishes its response to the guidance under §6;
    - **October 1:** every other system or institution of Class C or higher publishes its plan and progress report, in response to both the office's report and the public plans.
- **Proportional by class:** the planning burden rises with a body's effect on sentients, so it falls most on Class A and least on Class C:
    - **Class A:** a full plan covering all five horizons after each full Long-Term Outlook; every year, an update that shows what changed and why, or confirms that nothing did, and a full progress report; rated every year;
    - **Class B:** a full plan covering all five horizons after each full Long-Term Outlook; every year, a progress report, with an update only where something material has changed; rated every year;
    - **Class C:** a short-form plan in the published template after each full Long-Term Outlook, giving concrete steps for the 5- and 10-year horizons and a stated direction only where the body chooses; a short progress report every second year, with an update only where something material has changed; rated on each plan and report it files;
    - **Public bodies:** the governing authority and each Independent Body follow the Class A tier; any other public body follows the tier of its own class, or the Class B tier where it has none.
- **Phased start:** in the first full cycle after the office's first Long-Term Outlook, public bodies and Class A and Class B systems and institutions plan; Class C systems and institutions join from the second full cycle.
- **Setting the dates:** the dates above are defaults. An adopter may move them, but must keep the order and at least three months between steps.
- **Newcomers and changes:** a body that becomes required to plan, by reaching Class C or otherwise, publishes its first plan on its next due date that falls at least six months later. A body whose responsibilities change materially updates its plan on its next due date.

#### 7.4 Review and Limits

The office reviews and publishes what it receives, but the plan stays the body's own:

- **Review:** the office reads every plan and report it receives and writes a short public review of each:
    - **Evaluates plans:** checking whether each plan deals with the risks and priorities in the outlook that bear on the body, and whether its steps, timing, and resources could actually deliver what it promises;
    - **Evaluates progress reports:** checking whether the body has moved forward on the indicators its own plan set, not merely been busy;
    - **When:** the reviews of each cycle's plans and reports appear in the next Yearly Stewardship Report under §4.
- **Publication:** plans, reports, and reviews are published as **Type O** data under [Subcore 4-A §10.3 Type O](../../subcore_04.050_data_types.md#103-type-o-open-public-oversight-baseline-disclosure-data), following the standard data-type rules of [Subcore 4-A §10 Data Classifications](../../subcore_04.050_data_types.md#10-data-classifications):
    - **Typing first:** the body that writes a plan or report types its content under [Subcore 4-A §3 Determination of Classification](../../subcore_04.050_data_types.md#3-determination-of-classification), and where more than one type fits, the most protective one governs;
    - **What is withheld:** content of a type that is restricted or non-accessible by default, such as personal, relational, or identity data (Type H, Type I), inner-state data (Type N), security-sensitive material (Type S), or a sentient's own unreleased works (Type Y), is never published raw; Type G source records stay non-public but open to structured audit access;
    - **What is published instead:** the strongest feasible Type O substitute, such as aggregate, de-identified, summary, or delayed figures, under the substitute rules of [Subcore 4-A §8 Type O Baseline for Class A/B/C Systems](../../subcore_04.050_data_types.md#8-type-o-baseline-for-class-abc-systems);
    - **Holding back:** any withholding is narrow, documented, and challengeable, and the published version notes which type was withheld and why; no withholding may hide a material risk, a failure, or a lack of progress.
- **Limits:**
    - the office may say that a plan falls short and why;
    - it may not rewrite a plan, approve or veto one, allocate resources, or impose a penalty; its alignment rating under §7.5 is a published assessment, and any consequence of a low rating is decided by a forum, not by the office;
    - a plan is the body's own: the office may not use a plan or report to direct a body's lawful activity; and
    - an Independent Body's plan never overrides that body's independent judgment within its own domain.
- **Use in the progress assessment:** plans and reports are the main evidence for the progress assessment in §6.

#### 7.5 Alignment Rating and Forum Contest

Each review ends with an **alignment rating** on a five-point ladder, so that readers can see at a glance how well a body is planning for the sentients it affects, and so that a poor plan has a consequence that someone other than the office decides.

- **What the rating measures:** how well the plan and progress report answer the outlook and guidance, not whether the body agrees with them. A body that departs from the guidance with credible, published reasons can still rate well, so the ladder never limits the pluralism protected by [Chapter Twenty-Seven §3.1.4 Contestation, Correction, and Pluralism](../../core_27.000_governance.md#314-contestation-correction-and-pluralism).
- **The ladder:**
    - **5, fully aligned:** deals with every relevant risk and priority, with realistic steps, resources, and indicators, and progress is on track;
    - **4, largely aligned:** minor gaps or delays, each with a credible plan to close it;
    - **3, adequately reasoned:** deals with the main risks; any departure from the guidance is explained with credible reasons; progress is partial but real;
    - **2, weak:** a material risk is ignored or unexplained, the steps could not deliver what the plan promises, or progress has stalled without a credible reason;
    - **1, absent or empty:** no plan or report, a token plan, or no progress and no explanation.
- **Published with reasons:** each rating is published with the review that supports it, in the Yearly Stewardship Report.
- **A published rubric, checked from outside:**
    - the office publishes detailed criteria for each step of the ladder, with sample plans showing what earns each rating, and applies the same criteria to every body;
    - each year an outside auditor rates a random sample of plans and reports on its own and compares its ratings with the office's;
    - the auditor publishes where the two differ, and the office explains or corrects each material difference in its next Yearly Stewardship Report.
- **Contesting the rating:** a body may ask the office to reconsider its rating within a published period after the report, and may then contest it before a forum under [Chapter Twenty-Two](../../core_22.000_forum.md#chapter-twenty-two-standards-routing-and-forum-cases).
- **A rating below 3 opens the body to forum contest:**
    - **Who may bring it:** any sentient the body represents or materially affects, or the office itself; for a public body, any sentient;
    - **What the forum decides:** whether the body has failed its duty to plan for those it represents under [Chapter Two §17.7 Duty to Represent Stakeholder Interests](../../core_02.040_stewardship_capacity_principles.md#177-duty-to-represent-stakeholder-interests);
    - **What the forum may order:** a revised plan by a set date, a renewed hearing of the body's stakeholders under [Article XII-A](../../core_04.010_rights_part_b.md#article-xii-a-stakeholder-system-participation-and-representation), and, where the failure continues across two cycles, a record of it in the body's standing records under [Chapter Fourteen](../../core_14.000_standing_assessment.md#chapter-fourteen-standing-records--question-1-what-happened);
    - **What the forum may not order:** the content of the plan, or any step that directs the body's lawful activity beyond planning properly.

#### 7.6 Failure to Submit

A missing plan or report is never hidden. The office publishes every missing or late plan or report as such and rates it 1 under §7.5, and it has a further consequence:

- **For a public body:** it is a procedural failure that may be challenged under [Chapter Twenty-Seven §3.1.4 Contestation, Correction, and Pluralism](../../core_27.000_governance.md#314-contestation-correction-and-pluralism).
- **For any other body:** it is a missing item in its System Charter or Institutional Charter under [Chapter Five §3.6 If a System Charter or Institutional Charter Is Missing](../../core_05.000_charters.md#36-if-a-system-charter-or-institutional-charter-is-missing).

#### 7.7 Carried in the System Charter or Institutional Charter

Planning and evaluation in response to stewardship guidance is a standing item in each System Charter and Institutional Charter under [Chapter Five §5 What System and Institutional Charters Must State](../../core_05.000_charters.md#5-what-system-and-institutional-charters-must-state), so that it is part of how every chartered body describes itself, not a separate exercise:

- **A body that must plan** points, by Charter Pointer, to its current stewardship plan and its latest progress report, and names who in the body is responsible for them;
- **A Class L or Class P body** states whether it plans against the outlook, and if it does, points to that plan; saying it does not is a complete answer;
- **Upkeep:** a dead or out-of-date pointer to a required plan or report is a missing item under [Chapter Five §5.2 Charter Pointers](../../core_05.000_charters.md#52-charter-pointers), handled like any other missing item.

### 8. Stewardship Fund: Improvement Incentives, Public Education, and Basic Research

*In plain terms: the office has its own budget. It uses part of it to reward those who make technology cleaner, safer, or better, help the natural world recover, or make life in the community fairer and healthier. It also teaches the public about the long-term challenges ahead and makes the case for acting on them. It uses the rest to pay for basic research, the curiosity-driven science that does not pay off right away but has driven long-term growth. The office sets the goals and the rules; it never picks winners behind closed doors.*

The office runs a **Stewardship Fund** so that it can do more than advise: it can pay for progress on the long-term priorities in the outlook. The subsections below set the budget (§8.1), the three things it pays for: improvement incentives (§8.2), public education and advocacy (§8.3), and basic research (§8.4), and the rules that keep its awards fair and accountable (§8.5) and its reach limited (§8.6).

#### 8.1 The Budget

The fund has its own budget, protected from the reactions of those the office advises:

- The adopter sets the fund as a fixed minimum share of public spending, published in advance.
- The fund is set for each four-year outlook cycle at a time, so that it cannot be cut in response to the office's outlook, guidance, or reviews.
- Unspent money stays in the fund until the end of the following cycle, then returns to the public budget, except money committed to a prize under §8.2.
- The Public Finance Office holds and accounts for the fund, and its accounts are audited like any other public money.

#### 8.2 Improvement Incentives

The office may offer incentives for technological, ecological, and social improvement that answer priorities in the outlook or risks in the register:

- **Forms:** prizes for reaching a published performance target, commitments to pay for each unit of a proven improvement delivered, matching grants, and support for adopting improvements that already exist;
- **Examples:**
    - technological: cleaner or more efficient production, and more resilient critical systems;
    - ecological: restoration of damaged ecosystems, and a lower footprint under Article I-B;
    - health: targeted treatments and preventions for specific diseases, such as a cure, a vaccine, or a cheaper diagnostic, especially for diseases that markets neglect;
    - social: better public health, wider access to the survival floor, stronger communities, and less exclusion;
- **Prize reserve:**
    - a published share of the fund, by default at least one-tenth, is set aside for prizes;
    - money committed to a prize stays in the reserve until the prize is won or its end date passes, even across cycles, so that large prizes for hard targets, such as a cure, are credible;
    - a prize states its target, how a winner is verified, and its amount before it opens, and a winning treatment or prevention must be made available on fair terms to the sentients who need it;
- **Outcomes, not favourites:** each incentive pays for a measured result and is open to any sentient, institution, or system that can deliver it, whatever method it uses;
- **End dates:** each incentive program states its target, its budget, and an end date no more than ten years after it opens, and is evaluated in public before any renewal.

#### 8.3 Public Education and Advocacy for the Long Term

The office may help the public understand the long-term picture and argue for acting on it, within limits that keep it out of contested selections:

- The office may fund and run education on long-term risks and opportunities: teaching materials, courses, public talks, and plain-language guides to the outlook and the register.
- The office may advocate in public for the long-term priorities in its outlook, and may support others who teach about them through open grants.
- Advocacy argues only for priorities stated in a published Long-Term Outlook or Yearly Stewardship Report, with their evidence and confidence shown. It never argues for or against a specific body's plan or rating.
- Education and advocacy are run by a unit kept apart from the staff who review and rate plans: neither takes part in the other's work, so that a rating is never shaped by a campaign.
- Advocacy never supports or opposes a candidate, party, or faction in a contested selection, and it is paused during the quiet period before each vote, whose length the adopter sets.
- Anyone may answer the office's advocacy, and the office publishes significant responses alongside it.

#### 8.4 Basic Research

Part of the fund pays for research whose value lies in understanding rather than in any near-term product:

- At least one-third of the fund is reserved for basic research: open-ended inquiry that seeks understanding rather than a product.
- The office sets the share and the broad fields only, and ranks the fields by urgency, drawing on the risks and priorities in the outlook and register. Individual grants are decided on merit by review panels of qualified researchers drawn by lot, and the office may not choose, veto, or steer individual projects or their findings.
- Grants do not depend on any expected commercial or political return.
- **Unconventional research:** at least 5 percent of the basic-research share is set aside for riskier, unconventional work that does not fully align with consensus views in science and engineering:
    - **Its own panel:** these grants are decided by a separate review panel drawn by lot, which includes reviewers from outside the field in question;
    - **What the panel judges:** whether the question is clear and testable and the methods are honest and rigorous, never whether the idea agrees with prevailing views;
    - **Many small bets:** the set-aside favours many modest grants over a few large ones, so that more unconventional ideas get a fair test;
    - **Every result counts:** results are published openly, including results that fail to support the idea, so that a fair test settles the question either way;
    - **Same safety rules:** unconventional work keeps every safety, ethics, and Rights Floor protection that applies to any other research.
- **New teams and institutions:** a further 5 percent or more of the basic-research share is set aside so that new teams and institutions can establish a track record:
    - **Who qualifies:** a team or institution that has not yet received a research grant above a published size from the fund or from other public research funding;
    - **What the panel judges:** the quality of the proposal and the team's ability to carry it out, never the length of its record;
    - **A path to more:** a team that delivers what it promised has a published record it can rely on when it competes for larger grants;
    - **No gaming:** an established team or institution may not qualify by renaming or splitting itself, and the office publishes who received each grant and their prior funding.
- Results and data are published openly, except where publication would create a grave hazard to life or safety; any such withholding is described in public with its reason and reviewed at least every two years.
- **Review of results:** when a grant ends, a review panel drawn by lot rates what the work achieved on a five-point scale of worth:
    - **What is rated:** how worthwhile the results are, not how exactly they match the goals stated in the proposal; a sound change of direction is not counted against the work, and a clear negative result that settles a question counts as real worth;
    - **The scale:**
        - **5, major contribution:** changed understanding, or opened a new field, method, or line of work;
        - **4, substantial:** clear new knowledge that others are building on;
        - **3, solid:** sound and useful results, including a clear negative result;
        - **2, limited:** little new knowledge, or methods too weak to rely on;
        - **1, none:** the work was not done, or its results were not reported honestly;
    - **Kept light:** the review at the end of a grant is short, based on the researchers' own summary and published outputs;
    - **A second look:** because the worth of basic research often shows only later, a random sample of grants, together with any grant whose researchers ask, is rated again five years after it ends, and the later rating stands alongside the first;
    - **Published and answerable:** every rating is published with its reasons, and the researchers may respond and ask for reconsideration;
    - **Findings are free:** a rating never rewards or penalizes results for being welcome or unwelcome to any authority, faction, or consensus.
- **How ratings shape future funding:**
    - **Urgency first:** the fund's money is divided among fields by their urgency rank, so the most urgent topics always get priority;
    - **Track record second:** within a field, the panels deciding new grants weigh each applicant's past ratings alongside the quality of the proposal;
    - **New teams are not penalized:** a team with no ratings yet competes on its proposal alone, including through the new-teams set-aside.

#### 8.5 Fair Awards and Accountability

Every use of the fund is public, free of conflicts of interest, and judged by its results:

- **A separate fund board:** the fund is run by a fund board within the office, with its own seats drawn by lot under the same rules as the office's decision seats. The board decides incentive programs and fund shares; review panels decide research grants under §8.4.
- **A firewall:** no member or staff member who reviews or rates plans under §7 takes part in any fund decision, and no member or staff member of the fund board takes part in reviewing or rating plans. Neither side may see the other's pending decisions before they are published.
- Every program, award, recipient, and amount is published.
- No member, reviewer, or staff member takes part in a decision that concerns themselves or anyone with whom they have a financial, family, or professional tie.
- **A cap per recipient:** so that the fund cannot become a channel for one favoured recipient, no single recipient may receive more than a set share of the fund in any four-year cycle:
    - **The cap:** the adopter publishes the share in advance; by default it is one-twentieth (5 percent) of the fund for the cycle;
    - **Counted together:** awards to a recipient and to any body it controls, or that controls it, count toward the same cap;
    - **Prizes:** a prize won in open competition is paid in full even if it takes the winner over the cap, because its terms were published before anyone competed.
- The office publishes a yearly fund report, with its Yearly Stewardship Report, showing what each program and grant cost and what it achieved.
- Each Long-Term Outlook includes an evaluation of how well the fund's spending has served the long-term priorities.

#### 8.6 Limits

The fund gives the office money to spend, not power over those it pays:

- The fund is the only money the office may allocate.
- The office may not attach conditions that direct a recipient's lawful activity beyond the measured result being paid for.
- The office may not use the fund to reward or penalize anyone for the content of a plan, report, or submission under §7.

### 9. Long-Horizon Review of Proposals

*In plain terms: when a proposal would lock in effects for decades or forever, the office adds a long-term section to its evaluation.*

- **When:** a proposal evaluated by the Public Policy Office gets a long-horizon section when its material effects extend beyond 25 years or cannot practicably be undone.
- **What it covers:** effects on future generations, irreversible thresholds in the risk register, and the obligations the proposal would pass on.
- **One publication:** the long-horizon section is published as part of the Public Policy Office's evaluation, under that office's time limits and response rule.

### 10. Speaking for Future Generations

*In plain terms: the office may speak up for future sentients where today's decisions affect them, but it cannot stop those decisions.*

- **Submissions:** the office may make written submissions, in any forum proceeding or public consultation, where the interests protected by Article I-C are materially at stake.
- **No party power:** a submission does not make the office a party, does not delay a proceeding, and cannot block a decision.
- **Even-handedness:** the office speaks for future interests, not for any present faction, and publishes every submission.

### 11. Coordination Without Command

*In plain terms: the office makes sure everyone with public responsibility has a plan and reports on it, but it never writes those plans or tells anyone what to produce, buy, or do.*

- **What the office may do:**
    - require plans and reports under §7; and
    - offer incentives and grants under §8.
- **Where its power ends:** outside the office, it does not:
    - plan production;
    - allocate resources beyond its own fund;
    - set binding targets; or
    - direct how any institution, system, or sentient carries out its work.
- **Inside the office:** it plans and runs its own work, including the Stewardship Fund's programs.
- An incentive is an offer, never a duty. No one is penalized for declining one, and no public body may make an incentive a condition of any right or service.
- Its guidance is advice. No rule, contract, or funding decision may treat it as a command.
- More than one long-term strategy can be legitimate. The office's guidance never limits the pluralism protected by [Chapter Twenty-Seven §3.1.4 Contestation, Correction, and Pluralism](../../core_27.000_governance.md#314-contestation-correction-and-pluralism).

### 12. Records, Accuracy, and Challenge

*In plain terms: everything the office says can be checked, and it looks back regularly at how its earlier outlooks held up.*

- **Open records:** the outlook, register, guidance, assessments, plans, reports, reviews, incentive programs, awards, and submissions are published with their evidence, methods, and correction history.
- **Look-back:** each outlook includes a comparison of the previous outlooks with what has happened since, and what the office changed as a result.
- **Challenge:** any sentient may ask for a correction of an error of fact or method. Capture or manipulation of the office may be raised with the Integrity Forum.

### 13. Proportional Scaling

*In plain terms: a small community still needs someone looking ahead, but can share the work.*

The *Scaling* rule in [Chapter Seven §3 What Makes a Body Independent](../../core_07.000_independent_bodies.md#3-what-makes-a-body-independent) applies to this office in full. For this office it also means:

- **Sharing:** a small adopter may share the office with other adopters by published agreement, pool its Stewardship Fund with theirs, and run its basic-research share through a shared review panel.
- **Combining:** a small adopter may combine the office with its Public Policy Office, provided the long-term work keeps its own seats, its eight-year terms, and its own publications.
- **Lighter forms:** a small adopter may publish a shorter outlook and use short-form plans and reports.
- **Never scaled down:** the risk register, the plans and reports required under §7, the alignment ratings, and the governing authority's duty to respond apply in full.
