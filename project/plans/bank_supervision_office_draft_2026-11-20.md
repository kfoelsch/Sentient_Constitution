# CHAPTER [B]: BANK SUPERVISION OFFICE — SAFE BANKS, PROTECTED DEPOSITS, AND FAILURE WITHOUT BAILOUTS

Chapter [B] is the constitutional owner of **the supervision of banks and payment providers, the protection of deposits, and the resolution of a failing bank**.

### 1. Purpose, Role, and Scope

<br>

*In plain terms: when a bank fails, people lose their savings, and when a big bank fails, everyone can be hurt. This chapter puts one independent office in charge of keeping banks sound, protecting ordinary depositors, and making sure that when a bank fails anyway, its owners and lenders bear the loss, not the public.*

**Purpose:** Chapter [B] keeps the places where sentients keep their money safe. It does this through one independent office that licenses banks and payment providers, holds them to published safety standards, protects deposits up to a published amount, and handles a failing bank so that its essential services continue and its losses fall on those who took the risk.

**Role:** Chapter [B] is the constitutional owner of bank supervision, deposit protection, and bank resolution. Where it sits:

- The Public Monetary Office chapter sets system-wide financial-stability tools and lends as a last resort. This office applies those tools to individual firms and tells the Monetary Office whether a firm seeking emergency lending is solvent. Neither office does the other's job.
- The Public Debt Office chapter records any public money used in a resolution as public debt.
- The Public Finance Office chapter pays this office's floor and holds its reserve under Chapter Eight §11.
- The Public Information Office chapter publishes the financial-system figures this office relies on.
- [Chapter Twenty-Seven](../../core_27.000_governance.md#chapter-twenty-seven-constitutional-contract-legitimacy-authorization-and-stewardship) governs who holds governing authority, including the authority to set the protected amount under §8 and the levy formula under §3.
- A later corpus-institutions file carries the prudential standards, the intervention ladder, the resolution toolkit, the levy formula, and register fields. It may not narrow this chapter.

**Chapter scope:** The office:

- licenses banks, other deposit-takers, and payment providers, and keeps a public register of them;
- sets and enforces prudential standards for each licensed firm;
- supervises licensed firms and intervenes early when one weakens;
- runs deposit protection and its fund; and
- plans for and carries out the resolution of a failing firm.

The chapter's sections cover:

- why the office exists and what it is grounded in (**section 2**);
- the office's independence, appointment, seats, and funding (**section 3**);
- licensing (**section 4**);
- prudential standards (**section 5**);
- supervision, early intervention, and enforcement (**section 6**);
- stress tests and disclosure (**section 7**);
- deposit protection (**section 8**);
- resolution of a failing firm (**section 9**);
- working with the Public Monetary Office (**section 10**);
- no steering and no revolving door (**section 11**);
- the public register (**section 12**);
- audit and challenge (**section 13**); and
- proportional scaling (**section 14**).

**Out of scope:** The office decides no matter, finding, classification, or Standing Record. It does not set interest rates, issue currency, or decide public borrowing or spending. Fair treatment of customers in sales, advice, and lending belongs to the consumer-protection owner, not to this chapter; this office shares with that owner what it finds.

### 2. Why the Bank Supervision Office Exists

*In plain terms: banks borrow from ordinary savers and lend at risk. If nobody checks them, they take too much risk, and when it goes wrong the public is asked to pay.*

This section explains the reasons for the chapter. It is non-operative: the binding rules are in [§3](#3-independence-and-placement) through [§14](#14-proportional-scaling-and-adopters-without-their-own-currency).

**Grounded in:**

- Chapter One principles:
  - [§18.3 Separation of Duties](../../core_01.050_governance_incentive_application.md#183-separation-of-duties);
  - [§19 Incentive Alignment and System Capture](../../core_01.050_governance_incentive_application.md#19-incentive-alignment-and-system-capture); and
  - [§19.2 Convenient Proxies and Proxy Divergence](../../core_01.050_governance_incentive_application.md#192-convenient-proxies-and-proxy-divergence).
- Chapter Six Articles:
  - [Article V-A](../../core_06.000_rights_part_a.md#article-v-a-dependency-mapping-and-resource-flow-transparency) (*Dependency Mapping and Resource-Flow Transparency*);
  - [Article XIII-C](../../core_06.010_rights_part_b.md#article-xiii-c-conflicts-of-interest-gifts-and-benefits) (*Conflicts of Interest, Gifts, and Benefits*);
  - [Article XIII-D](../../core_06.010_rights_part_b.md#article-xiii-d-funding-integrity) (*Funding Integrity*); and
  - [Article XVII](../../core_06.020_rights_part_c.md#article-xvii-audit-transparency-and-independent-verification) (*Audit, Transparency, and Independent Verification*).

**The problem it answers.** Banks hold what most sentients have saved, and they fail in four familiar ways:

- **Too much risk:** thin capital, short funding, and concentrated lending, because the gains go to owners and managers and the losses go to depositors and the public.
- **Capture:** supervisors who look away for a powerful bank, or who move to a well-paid job at the bank they watched.
- **Runs:** depositors who fear losses all try to leave at once, and even a sound bank can fall.
- **Bailouts:** a large bank fails, and the public pays to save its owners and lenders because there is no orderly way to let it fail.

**Why this approach.** International standards for bank supervision, deposit insurance, and resolution, and the lessons of the 2007–2009 crisis and later bank failures, point the same way: licensing with fit owners and managers, risk-based capital and liquidity standards, early and forceful intervention, protected deposits paid out quickly from a fund the banks pay for, and a resolution regime that keeps essential services running while owners and creditors absorb the losses. The chapter turns those lessons into four design choices:

- **An independent supervisor** that no bank and no official can lean on, with supervisors who rotate and may not move to the firms they watched.
- **Rules before discretion:** published standards and an intervention ladder that starts early and does not wait for failure.
- **Depositors protected, owners and lenders not:** ordinary deposits are safe up to a published amount; everyone else takes the risk they were paid for.
- **No bailouts:** public money is the last resort, recorded as debt, and recovered from the industry.

**How it carries the Aims and the Tetrad.** Safe places to keep money protect the means of every sentient and keep one firm's failure from becoming everyone's, serving [the Two Constitutional Aims](../../core_00.000_preamble.md#two-constitutional-aims). Within the [Constitutional Tetrad](../../core_00.000_preamble.md#constitutional-tetrad):

- **Oversight:** published standards, stress tests, and a public register.
- **Accountability:** those who caused a failure lose their positions and bear losses first.
- **Participation:** the protected amount and the levy formula are set in the open by the governing authority.
- **Timeliness:** early intervention, and depositors paid within days.

These reasons do not add duties. They explain why the duties in §3 to §14 take the shape they do.

### 3. Independence and Placement

*In plain terms: the banks pay for their supervisor, but they cannot choose it, lean on it, or hire away its people.*

**Independent Body:** The Bank Supervision Office is an Independent Body under [Chapter Eight §11](../../core_08.000_functional_independence_separation_of_duties.md#11-independent-bodies), and meets every standard requirement there. For this office:

- it sits outside the Material Control Line of the governing authority, every forum family, the Public Monetary Office, the Public Debt Office, the Public Finance Office, and every firm it licenses or supervises;
- the protected decision-makers are the members of its supervision board and of its resolution panel, drawn by lot under *Appointment by sortition* in Chapter Eight §11 from candidates who pass a competency gate in banking supervision, financial risk, accounting, or insolvency and resolution;
- its decisions and releases can move asset prices, so *Market-moving information* in Chapter Eight §11 applies in full; and
- its floor is set under *Protected funding* in Chapter Eight §11 and paid through the Public Finance Office.

**Funding from the firms it supervises.** The cost of supervision is recovered from licensed firms by charges set under a published formula tied to each firm's size and risk. The charges are collected into the office's funding track through the Public Finance Office, never paid to the office by a firm directly. No firm may pay more to get a different decision, and no charge may depend on the outcome of a supervisory decision. A shortfall in charges never reduces the office's floor.

**Separate panels.** Supervision and resolution are decided by separate panels with separate decision-makers, so the body that judged a firm's health is not the only one deciding how to handle its failure.

**Seat map:** Every materially binding act of the office, such as granting or withdrawing a licence, imposing a requirement, intervening, paying out deposits, or placing a firm in resolution, places the four seats under Chapter Eight. The independent check seat is a reviewer outside the panel that acted.

**No dual roles (added for this office):** In addition to Chapter Eight §11, no one may at the same time hold a decision seat in this office and any seat in the Public Monetary Office, the Public Debt Office, the Public Finance Office, the governing authority, or a licensed firm. Members and staff may not hold or trade securities of any licensed firm, except through broad funds cleared in advance.

### 4. Licensing

*In plain terms: nobody may take people's deposits or run their payments without a licence, and a licence goes only to firms with enough capital and honest, capable people in charge.*

**Licence required.** No one may take deposits from the public or provide payment services to the public without a licence from the office. Doing so without one is an integrity trigger, and the office may order it stopped.

**Licensing criteria.** The office publishes the criteria for each kind of licence. A licence is granted only where:

- the firm has the capital, liquidity, and systems the criteria require;
- its owners of a material share, its board members, and its senior managers are fit and proper, judged by published tests of competence, honesty, and financial soundness;
- its ownership and group structure are clear enough to supervise; and
- it can be resolved under §9 without public money.

**Changes of control.** Acquiring a material share of a licensed firm needs the office's approval under the same tests. Approval is decided by published criteria within a published period.

**Withdrawal.** The office withdraws a licence where a firm no longer meets the criteria, obtained it by false statements, or has not used it, after notice and a chance to respond, unless delay would harm depositors.

### 5. Prudential Standards

*In plain terms: every bank must hold enough of its own money to absorb losses, keep enough cash to pay depositors who want their money back, and not bet too much on one borrower or on its own insiders.*

**Standards.** The office publishes and enforces standards for every licensed firm, at least on:

- **capital:** enough of the firm's own resources to absorb losses, measured against the risk of what it holds, with a simple floor that does not depend on the firm's own risk models;
- **liquidity:** enough readily usable assets and stable funding to meet withdrawals in a stress period;
- **concentration:** limits on exposure to any one borrower or connected group;
- **insiders:** limits on lending to owners, managers, and their connected parties, on terms no better than for others, decided without the insiders taking part;
- **governance and risk control:** a board that oversees risk, independent risk and audit functions, and pay that does not reward short-term risk; and
- **operational resilience:** systems that keep running and data that are protected.

**System-wide tools.** The office applies the system-wide tools that the Public Monetary Office sets under that chapter's financial-stability section, alike to every firm in the class the tool names.

**Proportionality.** Standards scale with a firm's size, complexity, and importance to the financial system. A firm whose failure would harm the system as a whole meets stricter standards, published as such. Proportionality never lowers the floor that protects depositors.

**Firm models.** Where a firm uses its own models to measure risk, the office approves and tests them, and the simple floor above still applies.

### 6. Supervision, Early Intervention, and Enforcement

*In plain terms: the office watches every bank closely, steps in early when one weakens, and punishes breaches the same way for every bank, big or small.*

**Risk-based supervision.** The office supervises each firm with intensity matched to its risk and importance, through reports, on-site examination, and independent review of the firm's own figures.

**Supervisor rotation.** The lead supervisor for a firm rotates at least every three years by default, so that no supervisor grows too close to the firm.

**Early intervention ladder.** The office publishes an intervention ladder with trigger points tied to capital, liquidity, and governance. As a firm crosses each trigger, the office must take at least the steps the ladder names, such as:

- a plan to restore the firm's position, with deadlines;
- limits on dividends, bonuses, and growth;
- replacement of board members or managers; and
- at the last trigger, placing the firm in resolution under §9.

The office may act sooner than the ladder requires. It may not act later, and a decision not to take a required step is published with reasons and is open to challenge.

**Enforcement.** The office may impose penalties on firms for breaching this chapter or its standards, and on board members and managers responsible for the breach, including removal and a ban from the sector. Penalties follow a published scale applied alike to every firm. Final enforcement decisions are published.

**No forbearance.** The office may not relax a standard, delay a step, or overlook a breach for a particular firm because of its size, its owners, its political ties, or the cost of acting.

### 7. Stress Tests and Disclosure

*In plain terms: the office checks in public whether the banks could survive a bad shock, and banks must tell the public enough to judge their safety.*

**Stress tests.** At least once each reporting year, the office tests whether firms important to the financial system could survive severe but plausible shocks, under published scenarios set with the Public Monetary Office. Results are published firm by firm, with the actions the office requires.

**Firm disclosure.** Licensed firms publish their capital, liquidity, and risk positions under a published template, so depositors and investors can judge their safety.

### 8. Deposit Protection

*In plain terms: ordinary deposits are safe up to a published amount, paid back within days if the bank fails, from a fund the banks pay into.*

**Protected deposits.** Deposits with a licensed deposit-taker are protected up to a published amount per depositor per firm, set by the governing authority by a published act on the office's advice. The amount is reviewed at least every five years and is high enough to cover the full deposits of most depositors.

**Prompt payout.** When a protected firm fails, the office pays protected depositors their protected amount, or moves their deposits to a sound firm, within a published period (by default, seven working days), without requiring them to make a claim where the firm's data allow it.

**The fund.** Deposit protection is paid from a fund held apart from public revenue and from the office's operating budget. The fund:

- is built in advance from charges on protected firms, set by a published formula that charges riskier firms more;
- has a published target size, relative to protected deposits, and is rebuilt after any use;
- is held in safe, readily usable assets, never in securities of a protected firm; and
- may be used only to pay protected depositors or, where it costs the fund less, to support a transfer of deposits under §9.

**Backstop.** Where the fund is not enough, the office may borrow through the Public Debt Office under a published act of the governing authority. The borrowing is recorded as public debt and repaid from later charges on protected firms.

**Public awareness.** Firms tell depositors, in plain words, what is protected and what is not.

### 9. Resolution of a Failing Firm

*In plain terms: when a bank fails, its essential services keep running, its owners lose first, its lenders lose next, and the public pays only as a last resort, and gets it back.*

**Resolution plans.** Every licensed firm whose failure could disrupt essential services has a resolution plan, prepared by the office with the firm, tested, and updated at least every reporting year. Where a plan shows the firm cannot be resolved without public money, the office requires changes to its structure or operations.

**When resolution starts.** The resolution panel places a firm in resolution where the supervision panel finds that it is failing or likely to fail, no private solution will prevent that in time, and resolution serves the public interest better than ordinary insolvency.

**Powers.** In resolution, the office may, by published decision:

- transfer all or part of the firm's business, including its deposits, to a sound firm or a temporary bridge firm;
- write down or convert into shares the claims of the firm's owners and creditors, in the order of their ranking;
- replace the firm's board and managers; and
- keep essential services running while this happens.

**Order of loss.** Losses fall first on the firm's owners, then on its creditors in the reverse order of their ranking, with protected deposits ranked above other unsecured claims. No creditor may be left worse off than it would have been in ordinary insolvency, and one who is has a claim for the difference.

**Public money last.** Public money may be used in a resolution only where:

- owners and creditors have first absorbed losses of at least a published share of the firm's total liabilities;
- the use is decided by a published act of the governing authority with reasons;
- it is recorded as public debt under the Public Debt Office chapter; and
- it is recovered over time from charges on the financial industry.

**Accountability.** Board members and managers whose conduct contributed to the failure lose their positions, may have pay recovered, and are referred for enforcement under §6.

### 10. Working with the Public Monetary Office

*In plain terms: the office that lends to banks in a crisis and the office that grades them work together, but neither decides for the other.*

- **Solvency findings.** Before the Public Monetary Office lends to a firm in an emergency, this office tells it, by a published finding (disclosed after the same delay as the loan), whether the firm is solvent.
- **Shared information.** The two offices share supervisory and market information under *Information handling and privacy* in Chapter Eight §11, through published channels.
- **System-wide tools.** The Public Monetary Office sets the system-wide tools; this office applies them to firms and reports how they are working.
- **No cover-ups.** Neither office may withhold from the other information that bears on the other's duties, or ask the other to delay a step to protect a firm.

### 11. No Steering and No Revolving Door

*In plain terms: no one may use the supervisor to favor or punish a bank or its customers, and supervisors may not cash in by going to work for the banks they watched.*

**No steering.** The floors of [Article XIII-C](../../core_06.010_rights_part_b.md#article-xiii-c-conflicts-of-interest-gifts-and-benefits) (*Conflicts of Interest, Gifts, and Benefits*) and [Article XIII-D](../../core_06.010_rights_part_b.md#article-xiii-d-funding-integrity) (*Funding Integrity*) apply to the office, its members, and its staff. In addition, the office may not:

- direct a firm to lend to, or refuse, a person, party, cause, or region because of who they support;
- use a supervisory power to reward or punish a firm for its owners' political activity; or
- time a decision to help or harm anyone in a selection.

**No revolving door.** A member or staff member who supervised a firm may not take a role, contract, or benefit from that firm, or from a firm that acquired it, during a cooling-off period after their last involvement (by default, five years), as **CI-27.205 §2** (*Role Criteria, Appointment Standards, and Removal Pathways*) provides. Former staff may not represent a firm before the office in a matter they handled.

### 12. The Public Register

*In plain terms: anyone can check whether a firm is licensed, how safe it is, and what the office has done about it.*

The office keeps a public register that shows:

- every licensed firm, the kind of licence, and its owners of a material share;
- every withdrawal of a licence and every change of control approved or refused;
- final enforcement decisions and the penalties imposed;
- stress-test results under §7;
- the size of the deposit-protection fund, its target, and every use of it; and
- every resolution, with the losses borne by owners and each class of creditor, and any public money used.

The register follows [Article XVII](../../core_06.020_rights_part_c.md#article-xvii-audit-transparency-and-independent-verification) (*Audit, Transparency, and Independent Verification*). Supervisory information about a firm that has not reached a final decision is kept confidential where publishing it would cause a run, but a qualified independent audit path must still exist, and the register shows that such information exists once the risk has passed.

### 13. Audit and Challenge

*In plain terms: outsiders check the office's work, and a firm, a depositor, or anyone else harmed can challenge a decision. The general rule is in Chapter Eight; this section lists what a challenge to this office may claim.*

**General rule.** The office meets *Audit and challenge* in [Chapter Eight §11](../../core_08.000_functional_independence_separation_of_duties.md#11-independent-bodies). In addition, the deposit-protection fund is audited separately each reporting year.

**Grounds.** A challenge may claim:

- a licence granted, refused, or withdrawn contrary to §4;
- a standard or requirement applied unequally, or forbearance, contrary to §5 or §6;
- a step on the intervention ladder not taken, contrary to §6;
- a depositor not paid in time or in full, contrary to §8;
- a loss borne out of order, or a creditor left worse off than in insolvency, contrary to §9; or
- steering or a revolving-door breach, contrary to §11.

**Timing in resolution.** A challenge to a resolution decision does not stop the resolution while it is under way. Where the challenge succeeds, the remedy is compensation, not reversal of transfers already made.

Under *Scope of review* in Chapter Eight §11, a forum does not set a firm's requirements in place of the office; it decides whether the office followed this chapter.

### 14. Proportional Scaling and Adopters Without Their Own Currency

*In plain terms: a small community may share a supervisor with others, but depositors keep the same protection.*

**Small adopters.** The *Scaling* rule in [Chapter Eight §11](../../core_08.000_functional_independence_separation_of_duties.md#11-independent-bodies) applies. A small adopter may share this office or its deposit-protection fund with other adopters by published agreement, provided each depositor keeps the protected amount and the payout period of this chapter, and the supervision and resolution panels stay separate. Where an adopter licenses few firms, it may use another adopter's office for supervision while keeping its own register.

**Adopters that use another currency.** An adopter that uses a currency it does not issue keeps this chapter in full. It agrees with the issuer of that currency, by a published agreement, how emergency lending and system-wide tools will work for its firms.
