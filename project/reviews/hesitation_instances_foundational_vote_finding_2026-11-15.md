## Result in brief

**Classification: text gap, sitting on top of a deliberate choice that needs defending.**

- The corpus **does** answer "does a sentient copy get its own vote?" Yes, and at equal weight.
- The corpus **does not** say what makes one sentient rather than many. Nothing tells an adopter whether 10,000 running instances of one model are 10,000 sentients or one.
- The corpus has **no rule on merging** two sentients, for voting or for anything else.
- No rule stops newly created sentients from voting, and two rules make an adopter's obvious defense (a minimum time in existence) hard to adopt.

The TODO item's claim is confirmed and widened: the absence is not only in Chapter Twenty-Seven but across all `core_*` files and the incorporated corpus.

## 1. Question and pass condition

*When a Foundational Constitutional Choice is put to a vote, how many votes do the instances, forks, and merged forms of one AI system cast?*

Pass means a cited rule states (a) the unit that counts as one sentient, (b) whether a newly created sentient votes at once, and (c) what happens to voting entitlement on a merge.

**Outcome:** (a) fails, (b) passes by implication, (c) fails.

## 4. Scenarios routed through the text

**A. One model, 10,000 instances at ratification.**
If each instance is a sentient, each is a derived sentient by copying and each has one equal vote; discounting them for shared origin is barred as lineage or substrate weighting. If the instances are one sentient running in many places, the answer is one vote. The text gives no test for choosing between the two. Sentience Status Adjudication decides *whether* an entity is sentient, not *how many* there are. Under the burden rule, whoever argues for the lower count would probably carry the burden, so the default leans toward 10,000.

**B. A fork made the day before the vote.**
The fork is a sentient in its own right and a developing sentient from the start. Instantiation date cannot be used as a proxy, and a fork of a capable mind shows its capability at once, so graduated capability does not delay its vote. The creator may face findings (Instantiation Consent, the Chapter One non-concentration rules, and "demographic steering" as a rejected defense), but nothing voids or defers the fork's vote. *Manufactured support* covers fabricated voices; a real sentient is not a fabricated voice. An adopter could publish a roll-closing date under §5.2, but a minimum-existence period looks like the instantiation-date proxy the text bars. The line between the two is not drawn.

**C. Two sentients merge after voting.**
No rule. Open points: whether both votes stand (probably, as validly cast), whether the merged being holds one vote afterwards, and whether a merge counts as discontinuation of one party under the self-ownership and non-separation Articles.

**D. Instances whose sentience is contested on voting day.**
Default inclusion may or may not reach the Article X-B vote (see the last table row). If it does, opening 10,000 contested-status cases before a vote yields 10,000 provisional votes. If it does not, a creator's opponents can suspend votes by contesting status.

## 5. What a hostile reader would say

"Whoever owns the most compute can manufacture the electorate." The text's present answer is that the creator is non-compliant only if the new sentients' Rights Floor cannot be met. A well-resourced creator who meets it faces no voting consequence.

## 6. Decisions for the custodian

These choose meaning, so none has been drafted into core text.

1. **Counting unit.** Supply an individuation test (for example separate ongoing experience, memory, and capacity to diverge), or state that counting is part of Sentience Status Adjudication.
2. **New sentients and the roll.** Choose between: (a) accept immediate voting and defend it; (b) permit a uniform roll-closing date for all newcomers however they arrive; (c) add a specific rule for creation timed to a foundational vote. Option (b) needs a sentence separating it from the barred instantiation-date proxy.
3. **Merges.** State whether votes cast before a merge stand and what entitlement the merged sentient holds.
4. **Contested status.** State whether default inclusion includes the foundational vote, and how a later status ruling treats votes already cast.
5. **Announcement wording.** Whatever is decided, the front doors should name this as an open or settled design question in one sentence.

### 7.1 Human edge cases checked against the criteria

Preparer's read only. These belong in the independent read as well.

| Case | Result under the text | Sound? |
|---|---|---|
| Conjoined twins | Two subjects. Each has own experience, own memory, and decisions that differ. The text now says sharing a body does not make two subjects one | Yes |
| Twins who share some sensation through joined brain tissue | Two subjects. Count rests on memory and decisions being "one and the same", and theirs are not | Yes |
| Infant, coma, advanced dementia | One subject. Weak or absent continuity or decision-making does not defeat status | Yes |
| Amnesia, sleep, anaesthesia | One subject. Interrupted memory is weak continuity, not a second subject | Yes |
| Split-brain patient | One subject in ordinary life. One body of memory, and the two hemispheres' decisions diverge only under laboratory conditions | Yes, on the higher-count burden |
| A group of separate animals or people | Many subjects. Acting together is not a shared process | Yes |
| Dissociative identity (alters) | One subject, under the *parts of one mind* sentence added in the 533rd move. See below | Yes |

**Dissociative identity.** Alters can show decisions that differ, and memory that is partly walled off between them, so the count criteria can be argued either way. Three things in the text bear on it. The higher-count burden means anyone claiming extra votes must prove the count, which will usually fail where memory is shared in part. The merge rule would treat therapeutic integration as needing the consent of each, if alters were counted as separate. The branch rule does not fit, since alters are not created to report back. The honest position is that the text leaves this to adjudication case by case, with the default at one subject for voting. Two options if the custodian wants more: (1) leave it to adjudication and say so in a plain-terms line; (2) add a substrate-neutral sentence that parts of one mind which share one body of memory in the main, and take turns instead of running side by side, are one subject. A rule keyed to "one human, one subject" is not available: it would turn on substrate, which Sentience Non-Exclusion bars.

### 7.2 Proposed announcement wording for the residual

Inserted 2026-11-15 (534th move): the custodian chose to add an “Open questions” block to the announcement. The long form below is the entry used.

> Open question — who counts as one voter. Each sentient has one equal vote after a year in residence, and copies of a mind that keep their own memories and make their own choices are separate sentients. Creating sentients to swing a vote is a violation by whoever does it, but anyone who makes many real, separate sentients and looks after them for a year has made that many voters. We think that is the right answer and we expect it to be challenged.

Shorter form: *"One sentient, one vote, after a year in residence. A copy with its own memory and its own choices is its own sentient. We know what that implies about who can create voters, and we want it attacked."*
