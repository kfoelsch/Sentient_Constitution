# Article XVII audit

Status: implementation review artifact (non-authoritative).  
Date: 2026-04-15.  
Scope: application of the Article-Principle-Definition audit rubric to `Article XVII: Standing, Reputation, and Participation Status`.

## Overall assessment

Article XVII has the correct constitutional function. It distinguishes participant standing from inherent dignity and stakeholder status, preserves contestability, and treats good standing as an auditable condition rather than an untouchable social rank.

The main issues were underexposure and anti-hardening clarity rather than doctrinal error:
- the article lead and `Article XVII-A` correctly separated standing from dignity and stakeholder identification, but they left too much room for standing criteria to drift into substitute tests for baseline rights or stakeholder existence;
- `Article XVII-B` preserved contestability and proportionality, but it did not surface reason-giving, interim-limit discipline, or the bar on using standing alone to choke off constitutionally required challenge, audit, or survival-critical access pathways;
- `Article XVII-C` was directionally sound, but it left current-evidence discipline, revalidation, and real restoration or requalification pathways too implicit, creating room for reputation hardening or stale-status governance.

Overall judgment: `publishable after targeted edits`.

## Findings

### Article XVII (chapeau)

- Operative claim: participant standing is a limited status used for selected governance or access pathways, not a measure of basic worth.
- Principle anchor: Chapter One `4. System Stability Enabler: Trust`, `5. Governance Principle: Freedom`, and `9. Interpretive Role`.
- Definition anchors: `Participant Standing`, `Dignity and Equal Moral Standing`, and functionally `Stakeholder`.
- Mechanics: the article lead should tell the reader immediately that standing is a bounded governance concept and cannot silently replace other constitutional status tests.
- Scores: Principle `3` / Definition `2` / Logic `2` / Constraints `2` / Publish `2`
- Finding: [Moderate] [Ambiguity risk] The lead sentence is accurate but too compressed. A reader could still underread how sharply standing must remain separated from baseline rights, stakeholder identification, and challenge or remedy access.
- Why it matters: standing language tends to drift toward worth-ranking or generalized exclusion unless the article shows the constitutional boundary on the page.
- Smallest close: add a sentence making clear that participant standing remains distinct from inherent dignity, baseline rights, stakeholder identification, and preserved challenge/remedy floors.

### Article XVII-A

- Operative claim: standing distinctions keep reputation- or qualification-based participation status from displacing inherent dignity or material-stake analysis.
- Principle anchor: Chapter One `4`, `5`, and `9`.
- Definition anchors: `Dignity and Equal Moral Standing`, `Stakeholder Participation Weight`, and `Participant Standing`.
- Mechanics: the distinction rule must resist proxy drift, especially when institutions are tempted to use standing labels as a shortcut for deciding who counts.
- Scores: Principle `3` / Definition `2` / Logic `2` / Constraints `2` / Publish `2`
- Finding: [Moderate] [Definition substitution] The subsection distinguishes standing from dignity and stakeholder status, but it leaves the anti-substitution rule too implicit. Readers can still miss that standing criteria may not redefine those categories.
- Why it matters: once standing scores or records become substitute tests for who has rights or who counts as affected, Article XVII stops functioning as a boundary and starts enabling caste-like governance.
- Smallest close: add a sentence stating that standing criteria, labels, or scores cannot redefine baseline rights or stakeholder existence.

### Article XVII-B

- Operative claim: standing restrictions must be challengeable and proportionate rather than monopolized, opaque, or irreversible by default.
- Principle anchor: Chapter One `5. Governance Principle: Freedom`, `6.1 Core Tradeoff Principles`, and `6.4 Rights-Collision Procedure`.
- Definition anchors: `Contestability`, `Proportionality`, and `Procedural Fairness`.
- Mechanics: effective contestability here requires more than abstract challenge rights. It requires reason-giving decisions, practical access to review, and interim restrictions that do not outrun their justification while challenge is pending.
- Scores: Principle `3` / Definition `2` / Logic `2` / Constraints `2` / Publish `2`
- Finding: [Moderate] [Underreach] The subsection already bars unilateral and opaque standing control, but it under-names the procedural features that make contestability real: stated reasons, functional correction pathways, and no broader-than-needed interim limits. It also leaves the standing-only bar on blocking constitutionally required access too submerged in Chapter Five.
- Why it matters: a formal right to challenge is often defeated in practice by unreasoned status degradation, indefinite temporary restrictions, or use of standing as a catch-all barrier to audits, remedies, or essential access.
- Smallest close: require reason-giving, time-bounded reviewability, narrow interim limits during challenge, and an express bar on using participant standing alone to choke off survival-critical or constitutionally required access absent adequate justification.

### Article XVII-C

- Operative claim: good standing may support trust-sensitive eligibility, but only as a continuously auditable status tied to current behavior and responsibility.
- Principle anchor: Chapter One `4. System Stability Enabler: Trust`, `7.1 Required Evaluation Factors`, and `7.2 Incentive Alignment and System Capture`.
- Definition anchors: `Accountability`, `Collective Accountability Failure`, `Trust`, and functionally `Trustworthiness`.
- Mechanics: good standing should track current, observable evidence rather than historical reputation alone, and the governance system should permit restoration where corrective conditions are met.
- Scores: Principle `3` / Definition `2` / Logic `2` / Constraints `2` / Publish `2`
- Finding: [Moderate] [Proxy drift] The subsection says good standing is subject to continuous audit, but it does not show clearly enough that trust-sensitive eligibility must rest on current, contestable evidence rather than legacy prestige, size, or inherited reputation. It also leaves restoration or requalification pathways underexposed.
- Why it matters: without current-evidence discipline and real restoration routes, good standing can harden into a stale caste marker that defeats trustworthiness logic and weakens corrective accountability.
- Smallest close: state that good standing depends on current, observable, contestable evidence and require real restoration or requalification pathways when material restrictions are corrected.

## Cross-cutting conclusion

Article XVII does not need expansion into a full governance code. It needs a slightly sharper constitutional surface so readers can see, without extra interpretive help, that standing is a bounded and contestable governance status rather than a generalized measure of worth, and that good standing cannot harden into an opaque or permanent social ranking.

Most important close points:
- keep the lead and `Article XVII-A` firmly anti-substitution;
- make `Article XVII-B` show what real contestability requires in practice;
- make `Article XVII-C` show that good standing lives or dies on current evidence and real restoration pathways.

## Suggested next-step edits

If this audit is used as a drafting basis, the smallest likely edits are:

1. tighten the article lead and `Article XVII-A` so standing cannot be mistaken for a substitute rights or stakeholder test;
2. revise `Article XVII-B` so reason-giving review, narrow interim limits, and access-floor protection are express;
3. revise `Article XVII-C` so current-evidence discipline and restoration or requalification pathways are visible on the page.
