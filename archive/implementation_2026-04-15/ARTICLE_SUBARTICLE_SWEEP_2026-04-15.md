# Article Subarticle Sweep

Date: 2026-04-15  
Scope: Chapter Nine owner files after the article audit pass  
Question: which already-split article subsections still carry enough distinct operative rules that they should be broken into additional subarticles for clarity?

## Core judgment

Most multi-bullet subsections should stay as they are.

The current Chapter Nine structure already does the main readability job by splitting articles into `Article X-A`, `X-B`, and so on. A subsection should be split again only when one block is doing more than one constitutional job, such as:

- stating a rights floor;
- defining a separate exception or carve-out;
- adding a distinct enforcement or remedy rule;
- adding a transition or implementation rule that readers will want to cite independently.

Multiple bullets by themselves are not enough. Many subsections use several bullets to express one coherent test, and splitting those further would overshard the chapter.

## Recommended split-now candidates

### 1. Article VIII-B

Current title: `Article VIII-B: Experiential and Derived Data Rights`

Why it should split:

- It combines an ownership baseline, a shared-event allocation rule, a prohibited-use rule, and a downstream handling rule.
- Those are separately citable ideas.
- The subsection currently reads more like a mini-cluster than a single operative floor.

Suggested breakdown:

- `Article VIII-B` or `VIII-B1`: experiential ownership baseline
- `Article VIII-C` or next available sibling: shared-event and multi-party retention rule
- next sibling: prohibited inference or misuse of others' internal-state-adjacent data
- next sibling: handling, publication, and accountability constraints for experiential data

### 2. Article IX-E

Current title: `Article IX-E: Security, Intelligence, and Covert-Power Limits`

Why it should split:

- It contains at least four distinct doctrines: anti-secret-police prohibition, exceptional authorization conditions, surveillance and internal-state limits, and oversight/remedy rules.
- Readers are likely to cite these independently.
- The current block is constitutionally important enough that a long bullet train makes retrieval and review harder than necessary.

Suggested breakdown:

- anti-secret-police and ideological-enforcement prohibition
- lawful authorization and emergency review requirements for covert powers
- surveillance, anti-laundering, and internal-state reconstruction limits
- accountability, independent oversight, and taint/remedy rule

### 3. Article X-C

Current title: `Article X-C: Adult consensual commercial sexual services and sexual exploitation`

Why it should split:

- It mixes a decriminalization floor with exploitation prohibitions, nondiscrimination protection, anti-circumvention rules, transition relief, and implementation routing.
- Those are not just multiple bullets; they are different legal functions.
- The subsection is also a likely future citation target in policy and implementation material.

Suggested breakdown:

- decriminalization floor for consensual adult conduct
- full exploitation and coercion prohibitions
- nondiscrimination and anti-stigma rule
- anti-circumvention and transition-relief rule
- implementation routing to institutions and systems corpus

### 4. Article XVI-D

Current title: `Article XVI-D: Innovation Reward, Disclosure, and Anti-Enclosure`

Why it should split:

- This subsection is effectively a compact constitutional IP framework.
- It contains distinct rules on reward baseline, exclusivity burden, expressive-work timing, classification scaling, disclosure and public-interest floor, domain carve-outs, and reclassification.
- Several of those rules are independent enough that they should not have to live under one citation.

Suggested breakdown:

- reward baseline and anti-enclosure principle
- exclusivity burden and time-boundedness
- expressive-work term rule
- class-scaled treatment and reclassification
- disclosure, public-interest floor, and carve-out domains

## Watchlist candidates

These are real candidates, but I would not split them yet unless we are already opening the surrounding article family.

### Article XXII-B and Article XXII-C

Why they are on watch:

- Together they carry the punishment test, capital-punishment exception logic, least-restrictive rule, prohibited punitive bases, and appeal/escalation protections.
- There is enough material here for finer subdivision.

Why I would wait:

- The two subsections already form a readable paired structure.
- Splitting further now may create awkward citation churn unless the whole justice cluster is being reorganized.

### Article XXIV-D

Why it is on watch:

- It combines deprivation/remediation authority, voluntary turnover incentives, essential-access guardrails, and implementation custody routing.
- Those are distinct enough that a later split could help.

Why I would wait:

- The transition article is already late-chapter and implementation-heavy.
- The current structure is still tractable if this subsection is not expected to expand much further.

## Practical rule for future splitting

If a subsection contains three or more of the following, it is a good split candidate:

- a primary rights floor;
- a separate exception or carve-out;
- a separate remedy or taint rule;
- a transition rule;
- an implementation-routing paragraph;
- a distinct nondiscrimination or anti-circumvention rule.

If it mainly contains several elements of one shared test, keep it as one subsection and improve local formatting instead.

## Recommendation

If we want a targeted next pass, split only these four now:

- `Article VIII-B`
- `Article IX-E`
- `Article X-C`
- `Article XVI-D`

Then reassess whether `Article XXII-B`/`XXII-C` and `Article XXIV-D` still feel overloaded after that narrower sweep.
