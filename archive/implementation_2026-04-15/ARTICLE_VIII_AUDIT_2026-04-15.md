# Article VIII audit

Status: implementation review artifact (non-authoritative).  
Date: 2026-04-15.  
Scope: pilot application of the Article-Principle-Definition audit rubric to `Article VIII: Likeness, Experiential Data, and Publication Rights`.

## Overall assessment

Article VIII is structurally strong. It correctly links outward identity, experiential data, and publication conduct into one constitutional cluster rather than splitting them into unrelated privacy, speech, or platform rules.

The main issues were publication-shape issues rather than doctrinal reversal:
- the likeness and reputation subsection was directionally correct but left contestable representation and identity-linked reuse limits too implied;
- the experiential-data subsection preserved shared-event ownership logic, but it was too compressed about non-exclusive ownership and too quiet about misattribution of shared events or inferred internal states;
- the publication subsection correctly deferred to the Chapter Five cluster, but it left the high-impact limit on even factually accurate publication too buried in cross-reference.

Overall judgment: `publishable after targeted edits`.

## Findings

### Article VIII-A

- Operative claim: sentients hold primary authority over recognizable likeness and over the fair representation of their observable conduct and accomplishments.
- Principle anchor: Chapter One `3.2 Truth`, `5. Governance Principle: Freedom`, and `6.2 Epistemic Disclosure Constraints`.
- Definition anchors: `Privacy (Informational)`, `Good Faith`, `Truth (Constitutional Constraint)`, and functionally `Contestability`.
- Mechanics: likeness protection should resist impersonation, deceptive attribution, and identity-linked reuse that attempts to stretch a narrow reporting exception into a general-use permission.
- Scores: Principle `3` / Definition `2` / Logic `2` / Constraints `2` / Publish `2`
- Finding: [Moderate] [Underreach] The subsection already protects likeness and truthful documentary use, but it left two load-bearing ideas too implicit at publication level: that representation should remain contestable where identity and reputation are materially affected, and that factual reporting does not silently authorize unrelated identity-linked reuse.
- Why it matters: without those clarifiers, readers can drift toward a weaker view in which truthful-looking attribution is enough even when challenge pathways are absent, or where a limited reporting exception becomes a general pipeline for model conditioning, ranking, or reuse tied to identity.
- Smallest close: add `Contestability` to the trace block, revise the reputation bullet to name contestable representation and resistance to false attribution or deceptive impersonation, and clarify that factual reporting stays tied to the reported facts rather than opening unrelated reuse.

### Article VIII-B

- Operative claim: each sentient owns their own experiences and experience-derived data, but shared events remain jointly ownable in account-giving and may not be used to invade others' protected internal states.
- Principle anchor: Chapter One `5. Governance Principle: Freedom`, `6.2 Epistemic Disclosure Constraints`, and `6.4 Rights-Collision Procedure`.
- Definition anchors: `Privacy (Informational)`, `Consent`, and `Protected Internal-State Boundary`.
- Mechanics: the article must preserve both sides of the rule at once: no one loses their own experiential data, and no one gains monopoly authority over shared observable events or a route to repackage inferred internal states as observed facts.
- Scores: Principle `3` / Definition `2` / Logic `2` / Constraints `2` / Publish `2`
- Finding: [Moderate] [Ambiguity risk] The subsection was conceptually right but syntactically compressed enough that the ownership rule, the non-exclusive shared-event rule, and the no-misattribution/no-internal-state inference rule were harder to see than they should be.
- Why it matters: publication readers should not need to reconstruct from surrounding doctrine that shared-event participation does not erase other participants' good-faith accounts, or that experiential-data ownership does not permit inferred internal states to be reported as observed fact.
- Smallest close: rewrite the opening ownership sentence as a full sentence, add a direct sentence against erasing or monopolizing another participant's good-faith account of shared observable events, and tie info-sphere disclosure of such data back to the truthfulness, attribution, and context duties in `Article VIII-C`.

### Article VIII-C

- Operative claim: good-faith publication is protected, but publication rights remain bounded by truthfulness, protected-data rules, internal-state limits, and high-impact harm constraints.
- Principle anchor: Chapter One `3.2 Truth`, `6.2 Epistemic Disclosure Constraints`, and `7.1 Required Evaluation Factors`.
- Definition anchors: `Good Faith`, `Truth (Constitutional Constraint)`, `Privacy (Informational)`, and functionally `Epistemic Integrity`.
- Mechanics: deferral to Chapter Five is valid, but the article should still make clear on its face that some distribution risks remain constitutionally constrained even where the content is not false in the narrow sense.
- Scores: Principle `3` / Definition `2` / Logic `2` / Constraints `2` / Publish `2`
- Finding: [Moderate] [Constraint drop] The subsection correctly pointed to the clustered definition, but it left the key doctrinal limiter too submerged: factually accurate publication can still be constrained when its primary or reasonably foreseeable effect is targeted harm, harmful coordination, cascading failure, coercive exploitation, or material degradation of epistemic integrity.
- Why it matters: if the article only speaks in terms of falsehood and recklessness, readers can import a speech-like absolutism that the clustered definition expressly rejects for high-impact or systemically dangerous publication channels.
- Smallest close: add `Epistemic Integrity` to the trace block and restate the high-impact limit in the article itself, including foreseeable harmful effects and the heightened proportional constraints for relevant `Class A` and `Class B` distribution systems.

## Cross-cutting conclusion

Article VIII does not need expansion into a full privacy, media, or information law code. It needs a bit more constitutional surface area so the reader can see, on the page, that identity control, experiential-data ownership, and publication freedom all remain bounded by attribution, contestability, internal-state protection, and foreseeable high-impact harm constraints.

Most important close points:
- make contestable reputation and non-exceptional likeness reuse limits visible in `Article VIII-A`;
- make the shared-event and anti-misattribution logic easier to read in `Article VIII-B`;
- make the high-impact publication limit explicit in `Article VIII-C`, including accurate-but-dangerous distribution cases.

## Suggested next-step edits

If this audit is used as a drafting basis, the smallest likely edits are:

1. revise the `Article VIII-A` trace and operative text to expose contestable representation and limit exception drift in identity-linked reuse;
2. rewrite the `Article VIII-B` ownership sentence and make the shared-event/non-misattribution rules explicit;
3. revise `Article VIII-C` so the article itself states the high-impact publication constraint, not only the cross-reference to the clustered definition.
