# Article X audit

Status: implementation review artifact (non-authoritative).  
Date: 2026-04-15.  
Scope: pilot application of the Article-Principle-Definition audit rubric to `Article X: Cooperative Interaction`.

## Overall assessment

Article X has the right constitutional logic. It correctly treats cooperative life as bounded by non-imposition, consent, revocability, verifiable collective-harm thresholds, and a narrow decriminalization floor that does not excuse exploitation or stigma-driven circumvention.

The main issues were publication-shape and visibility issues rather than substantive constitutional drift:
- the chapeau and `Article X-A` already point to the correct Chapter Five definitions, but the operative rule about informed, voluntary, and practically revocable association was split across short sentences that underexposed the actual consent standard;
- `Article X-B` had the right endpoint, but it did not say clearly enough on the page that offense, disagreement, or preference alone do not satisfy the collective-harm threshold;
- `Article X-C` was doctrinally strong already, but a few dense sentences made the anti-stigma and anti-circumvention rules slower to parse than they should be for publication reading.

Overall judgment: `publishable after targeted edits`.

## Findings

### Article X-A

- Operative claim: cooperative association, communication, and participation must remain free from coercive or manipulative imposition and must rest on informed, voluntary, and practically revocable consent.
- Principle anchor: Chapter One `5. Governance Principle: Freedom`, `6.1 Core Tradeoff Principles`, and `6.3 Freedom-Limitation Constraints`.
- Definition anchors: `Non-Imposition (Cooperative Interaction)`, `Consent`, and `Meaningful Agency`.
- Mechanics: the subsection must keep practical rather than merely formal consent visible. That means evaluative weight for dependency, power asymmetry, channel control, feasible exit, and revocability without unjustified penalty where feasibility permits.
- Scores: Principle `3` / Definition `2` / Logic `2` / Constraints `2` / Publish `2`
- Finding: [Moderate] [Burden/trace error] The subsection says the right things, but the operative consent rule was fragmented into a headline plus two short follow-on sentences. That made it easier to read as aspirational transparency language instead of a concrete constitutional floor for informed, voluntary, transparent, auditable, and revocable participation.
- Why it matters: if publication readers must reconstruct the consent standard across several short lines, systems can overclaim valid association on the basis of formal agreement while underplaying practical lock-in, obscured terms, or unjustified revocation penalties.
- Smallest close: replace the fragmented closing lines with one clear bullet that states the consent rule, preserves transparency and auditability of terms, and makes revocability subject to the Chapter Five feasibility standard.

### Article X-B

- Operative claim: freedom of action remains protected until a verifiable material harm threshold is crossed, and that threshold cannot be satisfied by offense, disagreement, or preference alone.
- Principle anchor: Chapter One `3.1 Safety`, `5. Governance Principle: Freedom`, and `6.3 Freedom-Limitation Constraints`.
- Definition anchors: `Collective Harm Boundary`, `Harm`, and `Materiality Determination`.
- Mechanics: the subsection must preserve Chapter Five’s verifiability and systemic-scope discipline and prevent collective-harm language from drifting into taste, stigma, or moral disapproval as a substitute for constitutional harm.
- Scores: Principle `3` / Definition `2` / Logic `2` / Constraints `2` / Publish `2`
- Finding: [Moderate] [Definition substitution] The subsection references the right definition, but it did not expressly reject offense, disagreement, or preference alone as sufficient thresholds. That omission left too much room for readers to import a looser community-disapproval rule than Chapter Five permits.
- Why it matters: Article X sits at a pressure point between association norms and enforceable limits. If the harm boundary is not visibly tied to verifiable material harm, it can be misused to justify coercive enforcement of moral preference.
- Smallest close: keep the current examples, but tighten the lead sentence so it states both halves of the rule: verifiable material harm crosses the boundary, and offense, disagreement, or preference alone do not.

### Article X-C

- Operative claim: consensual adult commercial sexual services receive a criminal decriminalization floor, while exploitation, procurement-through-exploitation, discriminatory stigma, and neutral-form circumvention remain constitutionally prohibited.
- Principle anchor: Chapter One `3.1 Safety`, `6.1 Core Tradeoff Principles`, and `6.4 Rights-Collision Procedure`.
- Definition anchors: `Consent`, `Protected Characteristics`, and `Substantive Fairness`.
- Mechanics: the article must keep valid-consent limits, exploitation predicates, anti-circumvention scrutiny, and transition relief visible without allowing later institutional implementation text to narrow the floor.
- Scores: Principle `3` / Definition `3` / Logic `3` / Constraints `3` / Publish `3`
- Finding: [Low] [Readability/structure] The subsection is constitutionally strong already, but its nondiscrimination and anti-circumvention branches were dense enough that the page made the anti-stigma and no-pretext rules work harder than necessary.
- Why it matters: this provision is likely to be read under political pressure, and clarity itself helps prevent later narrowing by implementation gloss.
- Smallest close: preserve the existing doctrine while tightening sentence structure so the protected-status floor, the independent-justification requirement, and the primary-practical-effect test become easier to scan.

## Cross-cutting conclusion

Article X does not need doctrinal expansion into a broader civility code or a separate morality-enforcement section. It needs clearer constitutional surface area where consent, revocability, non-imposition, and harm-threshold logic already exist.

Most important close points:
- make `Article X-A` show the consent standard as one coherent operative rule rather than a short stack of implied requirements;
- make `Article X-B` say plainly that offense, disagreement, or preference alone are not collective harm;
- preserve `Article X-C` doctrine while sharpening readability around anti-stigma and anti-circumvention.

## Suggested next-step edits

If this audit is used as a drafting basis, the smallest likely edits are:

1. replace the fragmented `Article X-A` consent lines with one tighter operative bullet;
2. revise `Article X-B` so the no-offense-alone boundary is explicit on the page;
3. lightly tighten `Article X-C` where scanability improves without changing doctrine.
