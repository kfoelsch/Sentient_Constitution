# Article-Principle-Definition audit rubric

Status: implementation review artifact (non-authoritative).  
Date: 2026-04-15.  
Scope: publication-readiness audit of how constitutional articles map to Chapter One principles and then to Chapter Five definitions, with supporting use of Chapters Two through Four where materially relevant.

## Purpose

This document defines a repeatable audit method for checking whether:
- article language is aligned with the principle or principles it operationalizes;
- principle-level claims remain anchored to the governing definitions;
- article logic does not broaden, narrow, bypass, or distort definitional meaning;
- trace relationships remain readable enough for publication without collapsing into a reference grid.

This is an audit-and-drafting tool. It does **not** change constitutional meaning.

## Core audit question

For each article or article subsection under review, ask:

1. What Chapter One principle or principle cluster is this text operationalizing?
2. Which Chapter Five definitions govern the key terms, tests, or constraints used in that principle?
3. Does the article preserve both the language and the logic of that upstream chain under full constitutional reading conditions?

If the answer to question 3 is not clearly yes, the text is not publication-ready.

## Direction of review

Run the audit in both directions.

### Downstream pass

`Principle -> Article`

Use this pass to test whether the article faithfully expresses the principle and does not drift from it.

### Upstream pass

`Article -> Principle -> Definition`

Use this pass to test whether the article's operative wording remains valid when traced back to the governing definitions and the Chapters Two through Four mechanics that control application.

Both passes are required. A section can sound aligned in isolation and still fail once traced back to definition mechanics, burden rules, anti-evasion constraints, or traceability requirements.

## Unit of review

Default audit unit:
- one article, or
- one article subsection where the subsection contains a distinct test, exception, conflict rule, disclosure rule, or decision procedure.

Do not force every paragraph into a separate row unless it carries distinct operative logic.

## Required source set

For each audit row, read these sources in order:

1. the article or article subsection under review;
2. the most directly implicated Chapter One principle section;
3. the controlling Chapter Five definitions;
4. any Chapter Two through Four mechanics that govern meaning, traceability, burden, observability, anti-evasion, ambiguity, or verification.

Minimum mechanical checks from Chapters Two through Four:
- joint O / E / C satisfaction;
- same-scope application across components;
- anti-evasion and anti-fragmentation;
- burden of proof and traceability sufficiency;
- observability and contestable verification where the article implies proof, disclosure, audit, or compliance claims.

## Audit tests

Use the tests below for every row.

### 1. Principle-fit test

Check whether the article states, protects, or operationalizes the same constitutional objective as the mapped principle.

Fail this test if the article:
- pursues a different aim than the principle;
- omits a limiting condition that the principle makes mandatory;
- imports a stronger or weaker priority rule than the principle allows;
- sounds compatible at the sentence level but changes the actual decision rule.

### 2. Definition-fit test

Check whether the article's key terms and implied tests remain consistent with the controlling Chapter Five definitions.

Fail this test if the article:
- uses a key term in a looser, narrower, or substitute sense;
- treats part of a definition as if it were the whole;
- relies on a proxy that the governing definition would not accept;
- silently changes the scope, subject, timing, or outcome condition of the definition.

### 3. Logic-chain integrity test

Check whether the full chain remains intact:

`definition meaning -> principle rule -> article operation`

Fail this test if the article:
- reaches an outcome the principle-definition chain would not justify;
- skips a required inference step;
- converts a bounded rule into an absolute one;
- turns a constitutional floor into a discretionary preference;
- turns a conditional permission into a general authorization.

### 4. Constraint preservation test

Check whether non-negotiable constraints and interaction rules remain preserved where materially relevant.

Focus especially on:
- Safety;
- Truth;
- proportionality;
- necessity;
- systemic evaluation;
- anti-capture and anti-evasion discipline;
- rights-collision handling;
- burden, traceability, and observability requirements.

Fail this test if article drafting reads as aligned but functionally weakens one of these controls.

### 5. Scope-and-time consistency test

Check whether the article uses the same functional scope and temporal frame as the governing principle and definitions.

Fail this test if the article:
- narrows from system-level evaluation to a local component without justification;
- treats point-in-time evidence as enough where continuity is required;
- ignores delayed, cumulative, aggregated, or cross-system effects that upstream text requires.

### 6. Publication clarity test

Check whether a careful reader can identify the operative rule without needing hidden assumptions or private interpretive context.

Fail this test if the article:
- is technically defensible only after extensive oral explanation;
- uses a term before the reader can reasonably tell which definition controls it;
- leaves conflict handling, burden allocation, or limiting conditions implicit when they are doing major doctrinal work.

## Scoring

Use a `0-3` scale for each axis.

- `Principle alignment`
  - `0` conflicts with the mapped principle
  - `1` loosely related but materially incomplete or distorted
  - `2` substantially aligned with minor drift or ambiguity
  - `3` tightly aligned
- `Definition alignment`
  - `0` inconsistent with controlling definitions
  - `1` partly aligned but uses terms or tests imprecisely
  - `2` definitionally sound with minor cleanup needed
  - `3` definitionally precise
- `Logical integrity`
  - `0` chain breaks or produces invalid outcomes
  - `1` chain is inferable but unstable
  - `2` chain holds with minor repair needed
  - `3` chain is clear and robust
- `Constraint preservation`
  - `0` materially weakens constitutional constraints
  - `1` constraints are present but under-specified
  - `2` constraints mostly preserved
  - `3` constraints clearly preserved
- `Publication readiness`
  - `0` not fit to publish
  - `1` publishable only with major revision
  - `2` publishable after targeted edits
  - `3` publication-ready

## Severity labels

Use these labels for findings.

- `Critical`
  - the article would authorize, excuse, or conceal a result the upstream chain forbids
- `Major`
  - the article materially weakens scope, safeguards, burden, reviewability, or doctrinal clarity
- `Moderate`
  - the article is basically sound but invites misreading, narrowing, or unstable application
- `Minor`
  - wording, sequencing, or trace visibility should improve before publication, but current meaning is still defensible

## Finding types

Use one primary type per finding.

- `Scope drift`
  - the article changes the governed system, population, or condition set
- `Constraint drop`
  - an upstream limit disappears or becomes optional
- `Definition substitution`
  - the article swaps in looser language for a defined term or test
- `Proxy drift`
  - the article treats a proxy or implementation marker as if it satisfied the constitutional objective
- `Overbreadth`
  - the article claims more authority or effect than the principle-definition chain supports
- `Underreach`
  - the article fails to carry forward protections or obligations the upstream chain requires
- `Conflict-rule error`
  - the article mishandles proportionality, necessity, precedence, or rights-collision logic
- `Burden/trace error`
  - the article implies compliance, disclosure, or proof without the burden, traceability, or observability structure needed
- `Ambiguity risk`
  - wording supports more than one plausible reading and at least one would weaken protection or validity

## Mandatory questions per row

Record explicit answers to these questions:

1. What is the article's operative claim in one sentence?
2. Which Chapter One principle section most directly governs that claim?
3. Which Chapter Five definitions control the key terms or tests?
4. Which Chapters Two through Four mechanics materially govern application?
5. What conditions, limits, or safeguards must survive from the principle into the article?
6. Does the article preserve those limits expressly, or only by implication?
7. Could a reasonable reader use this article to justify a broader or narrower result than the upstream chain permits?
8. Does the article rely on a proxy, local gloss, or implementation phrase that could displace the canonical definition?
9. What is the clearest failure mode if the current wording is left unchanged?
10. What is the smallest edit that would close the gap without overloading the text?

## Default workflow

### Step 1. Identify the article claim

State the article's operative rule in plain language before checking crosswalks.

### Step 2. Map the governing principle

Choose the primary Chapter One anchor, then list any secondary principle sections that materially constrain interpretation.

### Step 3. Map the controlling definitions

List only the definitions that actually do work in the article's language or logic. Prefer the smallest valid set.

### Step 4. Pull in mechanics

Add the relevant Chapter Two through Four mechanics if the row depends on burden, traceability, ambiguity handling, observability, verification access, anti-evasion, or scope integrity.

### Step 5. Test language

Compare key words and phrases across the article, principle, and definitions. Mark where language is:
- matched;
- compressed but still safe;
- expanded;
- narrowed;
- substituted;
- left implicit.

### Step 6. Test logic

Write the shortest valid inference chain from definition to principle to article outcome. If you cannot write it cleanly, the row is not aligned.

### Step 7. Record findings and fix direction

Capture severity, finding type, failure mode, and smallest drafting close.

## Decision rules for revision

Use these rules to decide what kind of edit is appropriate.

- If the logic is right but the wording is too loose:
  - tighten language at article level
- If the article is carrying doctrine that belongs in the principle:
  - move or restate the controlling rule upstream, then shorten the article
- If the article depends on a defined term but uses a competing gloss:
  - restore the canonical term or add a pointer rather than a substitute phrase
- If the article is overloaded by too many embedded safeguards:
  - keep the core rule in the article and use a compact trace or cross-reference where your publication style allows it
- If a fix would create new doctrinal content:
  - stop and escalate for drafting review instead of patching by implication

## Publication-readiness thresholds

Treat a row as ready only if all of the following are true:
- no `Critical` findings remain;
- no `0` scores remain on any axis;
- `Principle alignment`, `Definition alignment`, and `Logical integrity` are each at least `2`;
- any remaining `Moderate` or `Minor` issues have a bounded drafting close that does not require doctrinal redesign;
- the article can be read with its principle and definition anchors without hidden assumptions doing decisive work.

## Suggested audit order

Run the first full pass in this order:

1. articles with explicit collision, restriction, review, exception, disclosure, or emergency logic;
2. articles that operationalize Safety, Truth, Trust, Freedom, or systemic evaluation;
3. articles that rely heavily on defined terms with high drift risk such as consent, meaningful agency, accountability, contestability, proportionality, feasibility, dependency, risk, harm, and system capture;
4. articles already carrying trace blocks or known alignment edits;
5. remaining articles.

## What good output looks like

A strong audit result should let a reviewer answer, quickly and defensibly:
- what the article is trying to do;
- which principle authorizes or constrains it;
- which definitions actually govern it;
- whether the wording preserves the upstream rule;
- what exact drafting change is needed if it does not.

If the review produces long explanatory essays but not clear findings and close paths, the rubric is being used too loosely.
