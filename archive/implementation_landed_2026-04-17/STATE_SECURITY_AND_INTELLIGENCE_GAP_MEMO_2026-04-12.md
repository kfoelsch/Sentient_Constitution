# State security and intelligence gap memo

Status: Planning/review artifact (non-authoritative).  
Date: 2026-04-12.  
Question addressed: how much constitutional language currently exists around state security and intelligence, what is already covered indirectly, and what remains missing if the corpus later chooses to govern intelligence or security services explicitly.

## Bottom line

The corpus currently has **strong anti-surveillance, anti-coercion, anti-capture, and investigation-independence language**, but it does **not** yet have a distinct constitutional doctrine for **state security**, **intelligence services**, **counterintelligence**, or **secrecy-governed national-security functions** as their own institutional domain.

In practical terms:

- **Present:** surveillance limits, secrecy-constrained investigative safeguards, data-classification restrictions, anti-self-investigation rules, oversight/contest-integrity concepts, and strong barriers against hidden behavioral profiling or internal-state inference.
- **Partial:** an institutionally independent investigative-service model that could support some intelligence-adjacent functions if extended carefully.
- **Missing:** explicit authority boundaries, admissible purposes, oversight architecture, secrecy controls, emergency abuse constraints, handling rules for informants/undercover work, and dedicated anti-secret-police / anti-political-surveillance doctrine.

## Current coverage already in the corpus

## 1. Rights-layer constraints on surveillance and manipulation

The rights layer already prohibits much of what an unconstrained security apparatus would try to normalize:

- `core_constitution.md` **Article IX-A** identifies **persistent surveillance** as a form of manipulation incompatible with agency and self-determination.
- `core_definitions.md` defines **Surveillance Boundary (Constitutional)** and treats monitoring, tracking, correlation, and persistent observation as constitutionally limited where they materially degrade agency, dignity, or wellbeing.
- The same definition already requires evaluation of **purpose**, **intrusiveness**, **persistence**, **unavoidability**, and **power asymmetry**.

This means the constitution already has a strong floor against generalized or convenience-based surveillance even without a dedicated intelligence chapter.

## 2. Data-handling rules already block many intelligence-style abuses

The corpus strongly constrains the kinds of information practices typically associated with security-state accumulation:

- `corpus_primitives.md` bars retention that enables **surveillance**, **coercion**, **hidden power**, or reconstruction of protected internal states beyond justified need.
- `corpus_primitives.md` also bars defensive measures that impose **disproportionate surveillance**, **opaque enforcement**, or **security theater**.
- `corpus_systems.md` forbids use of behavior and activity records in ways that create unnecessary surveillance, coercion, latent reconstruction, hidden behavioral profiling, or persistent cross-context tracking.
- `corpus_systems.md` applies the higher-protection regime where data aggregation or modeling reconstructs internal states.

This is already a substantial anti-intelligence-abuse baseline at the level of data and system design.

## 3. Investigative independence is present, but not intelligence governance

The strongest intelligence-adjacent institutional material is in the courts/institutions layer:

- `corpus_courts.md` creates an **independent investigative service** that should sit in an **independent assurance** or **integrity** line rather than under ordinary police, security, or operational-enforcement command.
- That service may seek judicial or otherwise lawful authorization for **coercive** or **secrecy-constrained investigative steps**.
- `corpus_institutions.md` reinforces that the investigative service must remain institutionally distinct from operational enforcement command and final merits adjudication.
- `corpus_institutions.md` also creates **contest-integrity monitoring**, including attention to evidence availability under **security-constrained observability** and patterns of chill, capture, or retaliation.

This is meaningful infrastructure for integrity-preserving investigation. But it is still framed as **investigative independence**, not as a constitutional settlement of what intelligence or state-security services may exist and under what limits.

## 4. Secrecy is acknowledged, but only at the margins

The current corpus recognizes that some investigative or system-governance steps may involve restricted access or secrecy:

- `corpus_courts.md` refers to **secrecy-constrained investigative steps** and judicial authorization for intrusive measures.
- `core_constitution.md` references **security-sensitive disclosure balance** in the publication limits cluster.
- `corpus_systems.md` has extensive treatment of **classified systems** and information classification.

But the present use of secrecy is mostly procedural and data-handling oriented. It does **not** yet provide a constitutional doctrine for:

- what kinds of secrets may lawfully be kept by security organs;
- when secrecy expires;
- how secrecy interacts with due process, notice, challenge, and legislative/public accountability;
- how to prevent secrecy from becoming a shield for abuse.

## Major gaps if the corpus wants explicit intelligence/state-security doctrine

## 1. No explicit authorization theory for intelligence or security services

There is no current answer to questions like:

- May intelligence services exist as a distinct institutional form?
- If yes, what is their lawful purpose?
- Are they limited to external threats, anti-sabotage, anti-capture, or infrastructure defense?
- Are domestic intelligence and political-security functions prohibited, tightly constrained, or conditionally permitted?

Without this, the corpus has constraints on abusive methods but not a full constitutional theory of whether such institutions may exist at all.

## 2. No anti-secret-police / anti-political-surveillance rule stated directly

The anti-surveillance material strongly implies such a rule, but there is no explicit doctrine saying that:

- no body may operate as a **secret police** or ideological-enforcement organ;
- no institution may conduct political, religious, associational, journalistic, or dissident surveillance absent a very high threshold;
- protected reporting, opposition activity, labor organizing, journalism, or constitutional contest activity receive heightened shielding from security investigation.

That absence matters because direct anti-secret-police language usually does constitutional work that general privacy language does not.

## 3. No warrant threshold and no dedicated intrusive-measures taxonomy

The courts text contemplates warrants and secrecy constraints, but the corpus lacks a dedicated security/intelligence matrix for:

- interception;
- covert physical or digital entry;
- undercover infiltration;
- informant/tasking relationships;
- bulk collection;
- pattern analysis across populations;
- watchlists and threat scoring;
- emergency temporary measures with post hoc review.

Right now those categories are constrained indirectly, but not enumerated with tailored burdens and failure rules.

## 4. No oversight architecture specific to security/intelligence powers

The corpus has general oversight, independent assurance, contestability, and audit logic. What it lacks is a security-specific oversight stack such as:

- special court or designated judicial channel for highly intrusive secret applications;
- legislative or constitutional oversight committee with access rights;
- independent inspector/ombud with clearance-equivalent access;
- mandatory after-action review for secrecy-governed operations;
- structured disclosure and declassification review after risk passes.

For high-risk secret powers, the current generic oversight model may not be enough by itself.

## 5. No dedicated doctrine for secrecy duration, declassification, and affected-person notice

The corpus has classification language, but not yet a security-service-specific rule covering:

- maximum secrecy duration absent renewal;
- renewal standards;
- declassification triggers;
- eventual notice to affected persons where feasible and safe;
- minimum record preservation for later challenge, audit, and truth-seeking.

This is one of the most important omissions if covert powers are ever permitted.

## 6. No explicit rule for foreign intelligence, domestic intelligence, and cross-border cooperation

The current text does not distinguish:

- foreign intelligence collection;
- domestic security intelligence;
- counterintelligence;
- cross-jurisdiction intelligence sharing;
- participation in foreign partner programs;
- use of foreign-obtained intelligence to bypass domestic constitutional limits.

That leaves a major bypass vector if future adopters try to graft intelligence powers onto the corpus.

## 7. No doctrine for informants, undercover operations, or provocation limits

The corpus does not currently specify:

- when informants or undercover agents may be used;
- when inducement becomes unconstitutional provocation or entrapment;
- what protections apply to bystanders and protected association;
- what records and supervisory approvals must exist.

That gap is important because covert human-source practices are often where security powers become most abuse-prone.

## 8. No explicit remedy model for intelligence abuse

General remedies exist, but there is no tailored rule for:

- unlawful surveillance findings;
- invalid use of secret evidence;
- mandatory destruction or segregation of unlawfully obtained intelligence;
- notice, apology, compensation, correction, or institutional sanctions after covert abuse;
- derivative-use restrictions where tainted intelligence affected later action.

## Recommended insertion points if this becomes a drafting project

## Option A: Minimal constitutional floor, companion-file implementation

This is likely the cleanest architecture if the project wants to add the topic without bloating the rights chapter.

### Add a narrow core constitutional floor in `core_constitution.md`

Best fit:

- **Chapter Eight** if framed as institutional anti-capture / adjudication / authorization boundaries for covert powers; and/or
- **Chapter Nine** if framed as rights-protective limits on surveillance, secrecy, and political-security abuse.

Suitable constitutional-floor content:

- no secret-police or ideological-enforcement institutions;
- no persistent population surveillance absent extraordinary and justified grounds;
- covert and secrecy-constrained powers require independent lawful authorization, necessity, proportionality, narrow scope, time bounds, records, and later review;
- no domestic political surveillance, retaliation, or protected-activity targeting absent the highest constitutional threshold;
- secrecy may not erase auditability, challenge rights, or eventual accountability.

### Put operational detail in a companion corpus file or dedicated corpus section

Best fit candidates:

- a future `corpus_security.md` if the domain becomes large enough; or
- a bounded section split across `corpus_institutions.md`, `corpus_courts.md`, and `corpus_systems.md` if the domain remains compact.

Operational topics would include:

- authorization workflow;
- oversight bodies;
- covert-method taxonomy;
- secrecy expiry and declassification;
- informant and undercover controls;
- recordkeeping and notice rules;
- prohibited targeting categories;
- foreign-sharing and anti-bypass rules.

## Option B: Keep it narrow and anti-abuse only

If the project does **not** want to constitutionalize intelligence services as a normal state function, a lighter approach would be:

- keep the current general anti-surveillance architecture;
- add a direct **anti-secret-police / anti-political-surveillance** clause;
- clarify that any covert or secrecy-constrained investigative power is exceptional, time-bounded, and reviewable;
- prohibit foreign or inter-agency laundering of information to bypass constitutional constraints.

That would close the most dangerous loopholes without building a full intelligence-governance framework.

## Priority close list

1. **P0:** Add an explicit constitutional prohibition or severe restriction on **secret-police** and **political-surveillance** functions.
2. **P0:** Add a rule that covert or secrecy-constrained investigative powers require independent authorization, strict necessity/proportionality, records, and time bounds.
3. **P1:** Add anti-bypass language for foreign partners, inter-agency sharing, and use of third-party collection to evade constitutional limits.
4. **P1:** Add declassification / secrecy-expiry / eventual-notice rules sufficient to preserve auditability and remedies.
5. **P2:** Decide whether intelligence/security governance deserves a dedicated companion file or should remain a narrow anti-abuse layer.

## Suggested drafting questions before normative work

- Is the goal to permit legitimate intelligence functions under constraint, or to keep the constitution agnostic except for abuse prohibitions?
- Should the corpus distinguish **external defense intelligence** from **domestic security intelligence**?
- Is domestic political or associational intelligence ever permissible under this constitutional model?
- Which oversight body should own secret-authority review: courts, independent assurance, a specialist chamber, or a mixed model?
- Should affected-person notice be mandatory after risk passes, absent a renewed and reviewable exception?
