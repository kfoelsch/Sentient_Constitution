# Article XX: Justice After Verified Violation
*Plain-language version of Article XX (Justice After Verified Violation), including XX-A and XX-B in `core_06_rights_part_c.md`. Disputes and timely review are in Article XXV, and emergency rules are in Chapter Twelve §6.1. The official text remains the binding version.*

## The big idea
When someone has been shown to have caused serious harm, the answer is never revenge, endless delay, or cruelty for its own sake. The answer is a fair process that does three things: **stop the harm, repair the damage, and make it less likely to happen again.** Any action taken should be as small as it can be while still working, last no longer than needed, and be open to challenge and review.

This article covers what justice may and may not do after a violation is verified. Ordinary disputes and rights collisions go to Article XXV. Emergency measures go to Chapter Twelve §6.1.

## A. What justice is for
- Stop harm that is still happening.
- Make things right: pay back, repair, and change the behavior or system that caused the problem.
- Prevent repeats through support, rehabilitation, and safeguards.
- Put responsibility on the people who are actually responsible, backed by evidence on the record.
- **Justice is never allowed to be cruelty for its own sake.**

## B. Restriction Floors
This section sets the floors for serious restrictions, the minimum protections nobody can go below. The operating detail lives in other chapters (see the end).

### Serious restrictions
A "serious restriction" means limiting someone's freedom, access, role, movement, or resources, or leaving a lasting mark on their standing. It is allowed only if **all** of these are true at once. Meeting some of them isn't enough:
1. It is truly needed to keep people safe.
2. Repair or restitution is proportionate.
3. It includes rehabilitation or a way to reduce repeat harm, where possible.
4. It targets the right person, backed by evidence anyone can audit.

Also:
- It must target the specific person or role involved, not a whole group or a stereotype.
- A harsh label or public condemnation doesn't replace proof.
- **Locks:** A "lock" is a safeguard that closes off specific paths a person could use to cause the same harm again, such as certain roles, powers, or access. Locks count as serious restrictions, so they need the same four conditions above, plus a time limit, regular reviews, and a way to be lifted once the risk is gone. They can't stand in for repairing the harm done to victims.
- **Violence:** People who commit violence, or remain a continuing threat, **must** be imprisoned if that's what it takes to protect others. This includes people who turn down fair rehabilitation, and cases where lighter measures won't keep others safe. Imprisonment is not a standing lock, but it is recorded the same way (what is blocked, why, who is protected, how it ends) and it carries full criminal-process protections and a set time limit.
- **Anti-constitutional misconduct** (for example, trying to take over or block the constitution's protections) follows the "prevention locks" in Chapter Eleven §4.2, including imprisonment when it is truly needed to stop further harm. This is under the same fairness rules, and a final, reviewed finding must come first.
- **Killing is never a legal penalty or safety measure.** No emergency, category, or transition changes that. (This does not cover a person's own freely made choice under Article VII-D. If anyone pressures or repackages that choice into something imposed on them, the ban applies.)

### Lightest measure, with a clock
Every restriction must come with:
- a **time limit**,
- **regular reviews**, and
- **conditions for getting back to normal.**

Not allowed:
- open-ended severe restrictions,
- permanent measures when a reversible fix would work,
- restrictions with no scheduled re-check.

Basic equality (Article VI) and dignity apply the whole way through: when a restriction is imposed, reviewed, and carried out. People affected must be able to appeal, with more layers of review when a lot is at stake. They get **timely notice, the reasons, and real access to the record** so they can actually use their appeal. Only narrow, justified limits from Chapter One can restrict this.

## C. Emergency measures (Chapter Twelve §6.1)
The emergency rules now live in Chapter Twelve §6.1, not in Article XX-B. Any restriction an emergency measure imposes must also meet the floors in B above. An emergency can justify temporary action to stop imminent harm, but only if the measure is:
- time-limited, documented, and consistent with other rights,
- given an **expiry date**, a **set schedule of independent review**, and clear **rollback conditions**.

**No fake emergencies.** An emergency can't be declared for convenience, for urgency someone created themselves, to dodge normal scrutiny, or by claiming "existential risk" to get around truth-telling, audits, challenges, or time limits. The single exception is the narrow action strictly necessary to prevent imminent, irreversible harm, and even that must be traceable to a decision-maker, justified, and quickly reviewed.

**Keeping it going.** Going past the expiry date is not allowed unless someone documents that: it's still necessary, it's proportionate, and no gentler option will work.

**Contain now, explain and hear challenges soon.** "As soon as feasible" is not a deadline. People affected must get notice and a chance to challenge within these outer limits:

| Stakes | Latest deadline |
|---|---|
| Tier A | 1 week |
| Tier B | 3 weeks |
| Tier C | 2 months |
| Tier L | 4 months |
| Tier P | 6 months |

- The clock starts when the emergency measure begins, or when notice was put off, whichever came first.
- If an emergency delayed notice, treat it as Tier A (one week) unless a lower-urgency reason is documented.
- Restoring rights earlier is always fine. Going later is not, unless the "keeping it going" test above is met.
- A full independent review must start inside the same window.
- These numbers come from Chapter Twelve §6 and Article XXV-C. Other timing tables can't slow them down.

**Emergencies don't become the new normal.** Making an emergency measure permanent needs a Chapter One justification (necessity and proportionality), documented independent reauthorization, and, if it would change the constitution itself, the Chapter Seventeen process.
