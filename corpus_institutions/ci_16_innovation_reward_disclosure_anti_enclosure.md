**Purpose and owner boundary.** This section governs institutional handling of innovation-reward systems where constitutional adoption brings them within scope. Constitutional meaning remains in **Chapter Five** (*Innovation Reward and Anti-Enclosure*) and **Chapter Ten**, **Article XVII-D**. Implementation, cross-implementation, and burden discipline remain in `corpus_joint_structure.md` **CJS-5D.2** (*Implementation and cross-implementation interoperability, portability, and exit-integrity terms*) and **CJS-5A.4** (*Implementation and cross-implementation burden-of-justification and constraint terms*). This section supplies only the institutional registry, review, access-order, sunset, and appeal mechanics.

**Default institutional preference.** Where multiple reward mechanisms are feasible, institutions should prefer the least restrictive mechanism that can still sustain future innovation. That preference order generally favors attribution, milestone prizes, public or cooperative buyouts, levy-funded reward pools, pooled or standardized licensing, and compulsory-access tools before broad exclusion rights for high-dependency domains.

**1. Disclosure and registry.** Institutions must maintain a public, searchable registry for in-scope innovation-reward claims. Each entry must state claimant identity, covered subject matter, scope, duration, disclosure materials, interoperability or repair implications, and any asserted secrecy or access limits. Secret or materially vague claims must not receive constitutional enforcement benefit unless a narrow, time-bounded exception is justified under the owner sources named above.

**2. Reward proportionality.** Institutions must scale any exclusionary reward to actual contribution, disclosed novelty, development burden, and public benefit. Trivial variation, defensive paperwork, strategic bundling, or claims whose main effect is foreclosure of competition, repair, migration, or compatibility must not receive broad exclusion.

**2A. Classification-scaled treatment.** Institutions must evaluate innovation-reward claims in conjunction with **[corpus_systems.md](../corpus_systems.md), Chapter S2 — System Classification and Handling**. Claims affecting **Class A**, **Class B**, or **Class C** systems must face a strong presumption for access-preserving mechanisms and a weaker tolerance for exclusionary control than claims affecting lower-dependency systems. Review must consider dependency growth, coordination power, switching costs, interoperability burden, and whether the claimed innovation functions as shared infrastructure for the rest of the economy.

**3. Non-waivable public-interest uses.** Institutions must preserve non-waivable pathways for repair, maintenance, safety work, independent verification, research, education, archiving, migration, benchmark and compatibility testing, and reverse engineering for interoperability where those uses are otherwise lawful and proportionate. Contract terms, platform rules, or technical controls must not negate those pathways by indirection.

**4. Compulsory access and anti-warehousing.** Institutions must provide compulsory-license, access-order, buyout, or equivalent corrective pathways where in-scope claims are materially necessary for survival-relevant systems, standards-setting interfaces, public-interest implementation, or high-dependency transition and continuity. The same applies where a claimant withholds, shelves, trolls, or otherwise warehouses a claim in ways that produce artificial scarcity, coercive lock-in, or suppressed implementation without proportionate justification.

**5. Domain carve-outs and presumptive public-access regimes.** Institutions must maintain published rules identifying domains where strong exclusion is presumptively unavailable or categorically unavailable under adopting law. Those domains should include medicines and public-health essentials, survival-critical infrastructure, core interoperability or communication standards, foundational scientific knowledge, and constitutional safety, audit, or compliance mechanisms. Those rules should default to prize, pooled-access, public-buyout, compulsory-license, or equivalent public-access regimes unless a narrower temporary exclusion is affirmatively justified.

**6. Duration, review, and sunset.** Exclusionary innovation rewards must expire on a published schedule and remain subject to periodic review. Review must test continued necessity, disclosure sufficiency, dependency effects, downstream competition, and whether a less restrictive mechanism should replace the remaining exclusivity. Attribution may continue after expiration, but exclusion does not.

**6A. Copyright-like default term.** For copyright-like claims over expressive works, institutions should default to publication-based timing rather than author-life timing. The presumptive outer limit should be `publish+30` years from first lawful publication or equivalent public release. Institutions should adopt shorter terms, compulsory-license conversion, levy-funded reward, or immediate public-access treatment where works are publicly funded, dependency-critical, educationally foundational, safety-relevant, standards-like, or function primarily as cultural memory, civic knowledge, or shared informational infrastructure.

**6B. Reclassification review.** Institutions must maintain a published process for reclassifying innovation-reward claims when a previously lower-dependency innovation becomes a dependency-critical platform, protocol, model, marketplace, payment rail, or comparable coordination layer. Reclassification review must be able to narrow claim scope, impose interface and interoperability conditions, order compulsory access, convert the reward mechanism, or accelerate sunset where continued exclusion would conflict with the applicable **Chapter S2** class.

**7. Specialized review and appeal.** Institutions using this section must maintain a dedicated review lane or designated specialist panel for innovation-reward disputes, compulsory-access requests, anti-warehousing claims, and carve-out classification disputes. That lane must remain contestable, auditable, and structurally separate from claim-granting or revenue-dependent functions. It must also preserve secondary review and cross-institution escalation under **CI-6** (*Procedure integrity, contestability, and secondary review*) and **CI-8** (*Cross-institution coordination and escalation*).
