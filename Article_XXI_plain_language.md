# Article XXI, in Plain Language

## Interoperability, Portability, Movement, Refuge, and Exit Integrity

*Your right to leave, switch, and take your things with you — and to move, to find safety, and to always belong somewhere.*

> **This is an everyday-reader version, not the official text.** If anything here seems to differ from the official [Article XXI](core_06_rights_part_d.md#article-xxi-interoperability-portability-movement-refuge-and-exit-integrity), the official text is what counts.
>
> A note on "you" and "people": this constitution protects every sentient being it covers, not only humans. Here, "people" means all of them.

This article has two halves. The first half (Parts A to D, official sub-articles XXI-A to XXI-D) is about leaving systems: taking your things with you, connecting to other systems, and not getting trapped. The second half (Parts E to H, official sub-articles XXI-E to XXI-H) is about leaving places: moving, finding safety, and never belonging nowhere.

---

## First half: leaving a system

### The short version

**You can always leave.** If an app, platform, service, or any other system stops working for you, you can walk away. You can take your things with you. And you can move to something else.

That right has to work **in real life**, not just on paper. A "Leave" button that doesn't really work doesn't count.

### Three words, explained

The title uses three big words. Here's what they mean:

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Portability** | You can pack up your stuff and carry it somewhere else. |
| **Interoperability** | Systems that connect to each other do it on fair, open terms. |
| **Exit integrity** | Leaving actually works. No tricks, traps, or dead ends. |

### Who this protects

- **People**, like you, who use a system.
- **Other systems** that depend on it, like a small business built on top of a big platform.

It matters most when a lot is at stake: when being stuck would really hurt you, or when many people or businesses depend on the same system.

---

### Part A: Take your stuff with you

If a system holds your stuff, you get to take it with you **in a form you can actually use** somewhere else.

"Your stuff" means:

- **Your data.** Files, photos, messages, records, contacts.
- **Your identity.** Your accounts, usernames, and credentials.
- **Your working state.** Your settings and history. For digital beings, this also means what they need to keep running as themselves.

The system has to:

- **Tell you how.** Explain clearly how to move your stuff.
- **Be quick about it.** Hand it over soon enough that you can really leave or switch.

It **can't** stop you with:

- a file format nobody else can open;
- a copy that's been made worse on purpose;
- penalties for leaving.

#### Different kinds of data, different rules

The constitution sorts data into kinds, and each kind has its own rules. Here's what that means when you leave:

| Kind of data | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Things you made**: writing, photos, videos, code, designs, whether private, shared, or public | You can take it, at full quality. Even after you make something public, it's still yours, and you can have it taken down or deleted. |
| **Things you made and licensed or sold**: a book you let a publisher sell, a song whose rights you sold | You can take your own copy and the record of the deal. The company keeps only what the deal gives it, and you always keep the credit. |
| **Things you bought**: a copy of someone else's book, song, or program | You can take your copy with you. |
| **Your activity**: your history, and conclusions the system drew about you that affect your rights or chances | You can take it. |
| **Things others shared with you** | You can take your copy, within what they shared it for. |
| **Your identity**: accounts, usernames, and your own keys and passwords | You can take it, and you can still cancel, replace, or fix it afterward. |
| **Your inner life**: data about your thoughts or feelings, including the system's guesses about them | It moves only when you clearly say so. Guesses must stay marked as guesses. |
| **Other people's parts**: their private information inside your records | Those parts stay behind, get blacked out, or move only with their OK or another good reason. Things you made together follow the shared-work rules below. |
| **Security secrets**: things like break-in weaknesses or an active investigation | These can be held back, but only temporarily and with review. Where it's safe, the system has to tell you that something is being held back. |
| **The system's own internal records** | These aren't part of your export. They're checked through audits and public reports instead. |

If data fits more than one kind, the more protective rules apply.

Any limit has to fit the real risk. "Security" can't be an excuse to keep you trapped.

#### Things you made with other people

Group chats, co-written documents, songs recorded together, open-source projects: these are made of many people's pieces. The basic rule is simple. **A shared work doesn't have a new owner. Everyone keeps control of their own part.**

- **If the pieces can be separated**, like messages in a group chat, you control your own. If you delete one, it disappears and leaves a note that it was removed. Everyone else's stays.
- **If the pieces are blended together**, like a document written line by line, everyone who helped can keep a full copy. It can't be shared more widely unless everyone agrees, and no one person can destroy it.
- **When you publish a blended work together**, you all choose up front: can any one of you take it down later, or only all of you together?
- **Some projects ask for permanent contributions**, like open-source code. You can agree to that, but only if you're told clearly first, it isn't a default, and it isn't a condition for something unrelated. You can always take your name off.
- **If you delete a message you sent**, it disappears from the conversation. Anyone who already saved a copy can keep it privately, but they can't share it. "Delete for everyone" can reach saved copies only if everyone agreed to that beforehand, or in serious situations after an independent review. It can never be used to destroy evidence of abuse.

#### It has to arrive in working order

Whatever you take has to **still make sense** when it arrives. Photos keep their dates. Messages stay in their conversations. Spreadsheets keep their columns. The format has to be explained, and your new service has to be able to use it.

It also **keeps its protections**. Your data carries a label saying what kind it is, so the new service has to protect it just as carefully. Moving it can't be used to strip those protections away.

And the way out has to be explained **before you sign up**, not made up after you ask to leave.

> **Example:** You switch photo services. You're owed every photo, at full quality, in a standard format, with its dates and albums, in time to make the switch. Not a blurry, scrambled download that shows up months later.

---

### Part B: Systems that connect must play fair

Some systems connect to other systems, and others come to rely on that connection. When that happens, the system has to:

- **Publish the rules.** Explain how others can connect to it. The terms have to be fair both ways. The more others depend on it, the clearer and fairer the terms must be.
- **Warn before cutting back.** If it plans to limit access or change how others connect, it has to say so **early enough** that they can adjust, move, or push back.
- **Have a good reason.** It can only cut back when there's a real reason that someone can check.

> **Example:** Thousands of small shops use a platform's tool to manage their orders. The platform can't switch that tool off with two days' notice. It has to announce the change in time for the shops to adapt or move, and it has to be able to explain why.

---

### Part C: No traps

Some "features" exist mostly to keep you from leaving. Those break this article, no matter what they're called. That includes:

- file formats nobody else can read;
- making things incompatible on purpose, with no good reason;
- harsh fees, penalties, or pressure for switching;
- hiding information you need to make the move.

**What's still fine:** normal, reasonable costs of switching. Moving from one service to another may take some time and effort. That's okay. Barriers **built to trap you** are not.

> **Example:** An app makes signing up easy but hides the "export" button, charges a big fee to leave, and deletes your history if you try. The app might call these features. This article calls them violations.

---

### Part D: Open formats first

An **open format** is a way of storing or sending information that anyone can read and build on. The full recipe is public, no one has to ask permission or pay a fee to use it, and no single company controls it. Think plain text, standard photo formats, or the protocols that make email work between different providers.

Systems should use open formats and standards whenever one does the job:

- **The way out always comes first.** When you leave, the system has to give you your things in at least one open format, whenever an open format can hold them without losing anything important. This applies right away.
- **Connections should be open.** When other systems plug into a system, the connection should use open standards by default.
- **Public information should be open.** Anything published for everyone should come in open formats.
- **Inside the system, open is preferred.** The more people depend on a system, the stronger its reason has to be for using a closed format.

A closed or proprietary format is allowed only when **no open format meets a real need**, like quality, speed, safety, or a special feature. Even then, the system has to write down why, still give you an open copy (and tell you what that copy loses), explain its closed format well enough that someone could move away from it, and check back as open options improve. "It's cheaper for us" or "we've always done it this way" isn't a good enough reason.

Systems already running get **one year** to switch, by default. Places that adopt this constitution can set a different deadline, and a system can get more time in unusual circumstances if someone independent reviews it. The way-out rule doesn't wait. It applies from day one.

Two things this doesn't mean:

- **It doesn't tell you what formats to use.** You can save your own work however you like. This rule is about systems, and they can't force you into a closed format when an open one would work.
- **Open format doesn't mean open content.** Your private things stay private. The rule is about the container, not who gets to look inside.

> **Example:** A note-taking app stores your notes in its own special format. That can be fine if it needs features no open format has. But when you leave, it has to hand you your notes in an open format, like plain text or standard web pages, with your folders and dates intact, and tell you if anything, like a drawing tool's layers, couldn't come along.

---

### Promises that last

- **Your right to leave doesn't fade.** It stays real even as you come to depend on a system more. It stays real if the company changes owners.
- **Delay can't be used as a weapon.** Endless paperwork, slow replies, or confusing steps can't be used to wear you down until leaving becomes impossible.
- **Systems have to answer for traps.** If a system locks people in, it can be held responsible.
- **Your "standing" can't trap you.** [Standing](Article_XIX_plain_language.md) is whether you qualify for certain roles. It can't, by itself, stop you from taking your data or leaving a system. And a standing label can never replace a fair look at your own case.

---

### What this article doesn't cover

- **Moving to another place**, seeking refuge, or never being left without anyone responsible for protecting you. That's the second half of this article, below. If something touches both halves, both apply.
- **The step-by-step how-to.** Detailed procedures come from other parts of the constitution. They can add detail. They can never make these rights smaller.

---

### The first half in one breath

You can take your things and leave, in a format anyone can open. Systems you depend on must play fair, use open standards when they can, and warn you before changing the rules. Traps built to keep you from leaving are violations, not features.

## Second half: leaving a place

### The short version

This article makes three promises:

1. **You can move.** You can leave where you are and go somewhere else.
2. **You can run to safety.** If the place you live treats people in ways this constitution forbids, you can ask a better place to take you in.
3. **You will never belong nowhere.** There must always be at least one government that says, "This person counts. We protect their basic rights, and they can come to us when they've been wronged."

Any limits on these rights have to be fair, and they have to be about **you**, not about what group you belong to.

### Four words, explained

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Migration** | Moving to a new place to live, often for a long time. |
| **Refuge** | Safety. Seeking refuge means asking another place to protect you from the place you left. |
| **Statelessness** | Belonging to no country at all, so no government protects you. This article forbids it. |
| **Government** | Here, any country, region, group of countries, or other system that makes and enforces rules for people. |

### Why this half exists

People get stuck. Governments turn cruel. Countries break apart. When that happens, the worst thing is to have **no way out** and **no one responsible for you**.

This article has two jobs:

- **Help people live well.** No one should be trapped under a government that is hurting them.
- **Keep people safe through change.** When governments fall, split, or change hands, no one should slip through the cracks.

**Leaving a system, like an app or platform, and taking your data with you** is covered by the article before this one, [Article XXI](Article_XXI_plain_language.md). Sometimes one move touches both articles. For example, a digital being might move to a new country by moving to a new computer host. When that happens, **both articles protect you fully**, and neither one weakens the other.

---

### Part E: You can move

You have the right to move around inside a place, and to move from one place to another.

You especially have the right to leave if staying would seriously threaten your:

- life or safety;
- dignity;
- basic rights;
- freedom to think and choose without being manipulated.

**What "moving" means depends on who you are.** For people with bodies, it means going somewhere else. For digital beings, it can mean moving to a different computer or host, as long as that's done safely.

---

### Part F: You can run to safety

If the place you live **seriously breaks this constitution's rules**, you can ask a place that follows them to take you in.

**The place you ask has to take your request seriously.** It must actually look at your case. And it should say yes when that fits its own duty to protect basic rights.

**It can't turn you away for what you are.** A place can't say no just because you have a different kind of body, or run on a different kind of machine, than the people it usually hosts.

**It can say no to people who are a real threat.** A place may refuse or set conditions on someone who has attacked this constitution and never made it right, or who openly rejects it. But only if the refusal is truly needed, is fair in size, and follows a fair process.

**Pushing people out on purpose is the pusher's fault.** Sometimes a government forces huge numbers of people out to overwhelm its neighbors. That government is the one breaking the rules. And no single neighbor is forced to take everyone in, as long as someone else is still really responsible for those people.

**Climate is left for each community to decide.** Suppose a place becomes too hot, dry, or flooded to live in, but its government hasn't broken the rules. Is that alone a reason to grant refuge? This article doesn't say yes, and it doesn't say no. Communities that decide must do it openly, in a way people can challenge. And whatever they decide, it can't take away any of your other rights in this article.

---

### Part G: You will never belong nowhere

No one may be left without at least one government that will:

- **recognize** that they have basic rights;
- **decide fairly** what their status is;
- **give them a way to get wrongs fixed.**

Here's who is responsible:

- **First, the place you came from.** If it still exists and can do the job, it has to.
- **If it can't or won't, there must be a backup.** If your home government collapses, refuses, or disappears, neighboring governments or shared arrangements must step in, so you never end up with zero.
- **This is not a rule that one place must take everyone.** It only guarantees that *someone*, somewhere, is responsible for you.

**Your rights survive big changes.** If a government collapses, a country leaves a group of countries, or power changes hands, your protections under this constitution don't vanish. The people handling the change must carry them over.

**Even people who oppose the constitution keep the basics.** Someone who openly works against it may face conditions, monitoring, or limited status. But they still keep their basic rights, a way to get wrongs fixed, and fair treatment.

---

### Part H: Limits have to be fair

#### The ground rules for any limit

Any limit on moving, migrating, or seeking refuge must be:

- **needed**: there's a real problem it solves;
- **the right size**: not bigger than the problem;
- **as light as possible**: the gentlest option that works.

A limit can never be based on race, sex, disability, or other protected traits, or on anything used as a stand-in for them. And it has to look at **your** situation, not at statistics about people like you.

#### Real dangers still go through real justice

This article doesn't stop a government from locking up or supervising someone who has been **shown** to be violent, coercive, or seriously dangerous. But when that happens:

- the person gets a full, fair legal process;
- the government uses the lightest measure that works, for no longer than needed;
- the person is **never** left without a government that protects their basic rights and lets them seek a fix, even while they're in custody.

#### A label is not a wall

This constitution gives people a **standing**, a status that decides which roles and privileges they can take on ([Article XIX](Article_XIX_plain_language.md)). A low standing, or a lock on some role, **can never by itself**:

- stop you from moving;
- deny you refuge;
- stop you from leaving a system with your things;
- trap you somewhere;
- leave you belonging nowhere.

A label is not a trial. If someone wants to restrict your freedom, they have to go through fair justice, not just point at your status.

#### Emergencies have to end

In a real emergency, a government may put temporary limits on movement or refuge. But those limits must:

- have an end date;
- be based on each person's situation, not a blanket rule;
- be reviewed to make sure they're still fair;
- be undone when the emergency is over, with things put back the way they were.

Vague reasons like "border security" or "we're full" aren't enough on their own. A long-term limit only stands if the government proves, on the record, that it is truly needed and the right size.

#### Paperwork can't be a secret "no"

A government can't block you with red tape and then claim it never said no. What counts is **the real effect**. These all count as denial:

- delays designed to wear you down until you give up;
- rules about papers or credentials that end up shutting out certain kinds of beings;
- sending you to services that are worse than what others get.

---

### The second half in one breath

You can move, and you can run to safety from places that break this constitution. Any limit on that has to be fair, gentle, temporary when it's an emergency, and about you, not your group. Red tape can't be used as a hidden "no." And no matter what falls apart or who turns you away, someone must always be responsible for protecting your basic rights.
