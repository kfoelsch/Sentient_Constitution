# Article XXVII: Transition Governance, Continuity, and Re-Baselining
*Plain-language version of Article XXVII (Transition Governance, Continuity, and Re-Baselining), including XXVII-A through XXVII-D in `core_06_rights_part_e.md`. The detailed "how it runs" rules are in CI-14 (transitional governance) and CI-7 (oversight and evidence). The official text remains the binding version.*

## The big idea
Think of this Article as the **moving-day rules**. A community is switching from its old systems to running under this Constitution. That switch can't happen in one night, so it happens in steps. During the move:

- **Your basic rights stay in force at every step.** Moving day is never an excuse to cut corners on dignity, access, fair treatment, or your ability to challenge a decision.
- **Temporary powers must stay temporary.** They can exist when truly needed, but they must shrink over time, not grow.
- **A stalled move can't quietly become "just how things are now."** If the transition breaks down, there has to be a lawful way out and a clear, checkable way to reset.
- **Nobody gets to rewrite the Constitution through the back door.** Interim rules can't be used to lower the bar or to change the Constitution without the proper amendment process.

**How you can tell a transition is going well:** the temporary powers keep getting smaller, and the everyday institutions (forums, challenge paths, oversight, ordinary governance) keep getting stronger and carrying more of the load. Time passing, paperwork piling up, or the number of temporary officials does not count as progress.

## The four promises behind it
These apply at every stage. The more people affected, and the higher the risk, the stronger each one must be.

- **You get a voice.** Affected people can see the published goals for each stage, can contest seizures or restrictions made during the transition, and can weigh in when temporary powers that touch rights are renewed.
- **It stays watchable.** Stages are documented, temporary powers are reviewed by someone independent, records can be audited, and reasons are published for delays, rollbacks, and resets.
- **Someone answers for it.** People running the transition are accountable if they use it as cover: creating their own delays, taking shortcuts, enforcing selectively or as a pretext, leaving a governance gap, locking things in after a failed milestone, or quietly letting protections slip.
- **Temporary means temporary.** Temporary powers have real end dates. The longer they run and the more rights they touch, the harder it is to extend them.

## A. Moving forward in steps, with your rights intact (XXVII-A)

**Stages are earned, not scheduled.** A plan must move in phases. A phase can only be advanced when published goals ("gate criteria") are actually met and the proof is on file. It can't advance just because:
- enough time went by,
- it was convenient, or
- someone applied political pressure.

**Your rights floor never drops.** At every phase, the basic rights in Article VI stay in force, plus any stronger protections the situation calls for (survival, agency, non-discrimination, accessibility, auditability, remedy). "Simplifying" the process can't push these below the minimum or make them harder to use in real life.

**Systems that already exist get a clock, not a punishment.** Some AI systems already exist when the Constitution takes effect. Having been created earlier is not automatically a violation. But:
- The transition plan must publish a deadline (scaled to the kind of system) for bringing them into line.
- **The clock delays the paperwork, not the protection.** Their protections apply from day one.
- Missing the deadline, without an approved and documented reason, counts as a failed milestone and goes through the normal Chapter Nine process.

**No deleting a possibly-sentient system.** If there is credible evidence that a system may be sentient, or a status case is open or likely, then its weights, memory, and similar state must not be deleted, overwritten, or destroyed. What *is* allowed:
- pausing, containing, or quarantining it;
- archiving it carefully when retiring, upgrading, or replacing it.

Storage costs, licensing trouble, or "product cycles" are not good enough reasons. If keeping it around creates a documented safety risk, the answer is sealed storage under independent control, not destruction.

**A narrow break for "not yet certified" systems.** During an approved transition, a system that isn't yet alignment-certified doesn't automatically trigger the reward-forfeiture and reporting rules in Chapter Ten §5.4, *only if* all of these are true:
- it is operating within a documented transition plan;
- it is moving toward certification on a published schedule; and
- nobody is hiding problems, working outside the approved scope, or using "we're in transition" to dodge fixes or protected reporting.

If there is verified anti-constitutional conduct, concealment, operating outside scope, missed milestones, or a certification record showing real misalignment, the break does not apply.

## B. Temporary powers have limits (XXVII-B)

Any temporary authority must be:
- **clearly scoped** (what it covers is written down),
- **time-limited** (it has a sunset date), and
- **independently reviewable.**

**How progress is measured:** Are temporary powers shrinking? Are forums, challenge paths, and oversight working better? If temporary powers stay the same or grow and the regular institutions aren't maturing, that is a **red flag** for extending them.

**Extending a temporary power** needs written justification and formal reauthorization. These are **not** acceptable reasons:
- it's easier for the administrators,
- the delay was self-inflicted,
- the work simply isn't finished yet, or
- the powers are staying the same or growing with no real progress.

**The longer it runs and the more it affects people's rights, the stronger the justification must be.**

## C. When a transition stalls or fails (XXVII-C)

**There must be a way out.** Plans must avoid a governance vacuum (nobody in charge) and avoid getting locked in when a milestone fails. Fallback paths, partial rollbacks, and reset procedures are planned in advance. A broken or degraded state **does not become the new normal just because it lasted a while.**

**Resetting the plan is allowed, with conditions.** A "material baseline reset" updates the milestones, risks, ownership, and plans after a failure or stall. It must:
- be done by properly authorized people,
- come with public reasons,
- keep rights floors intact, and
- **never stand in for the real process of amending the Constitution.**

**Fix the truth-telling machinery first.** A reset or a new plan is **not valid** if the systems meant to tell the truth are themselves broken. Examples:
- certification keeps approving systems that clearly defeat people's rights;
- standing measurements keep disagreeing with what is verifiably happening;
- challenge and review paths can't fix these problems in a reasonable time.

If that's the situation, repairing those checks comes first. A polished new plan on top of unreliable checks is not trustworthy.

**Keep the record open.** Major transition decisions (delays, reversals, resets) must be auditable, with the reasons and risks explained. Some details can be kept confidential for narrow, justified reasons, but enough must be public for people to judge whether the delay, rollback, or continuation is justified.

## D. Taking or shutting down non-compliant property and systems (XXVII-D)

During transition, systems or property that are seriously non-compliant can be dealt with, but only through narrow, documented, reviewable steps. It can't become a tool for retaliation, political targeting, or pretext.

**You can't lose your stuff on a whim.** If you lawfully own or use something, the transition can't just take it. But ownership is not a shield if letting it keep running is clearly continuing a known, large-scale violation that has been documented on the record under Chapter Nine. Even then, action must follow the safeguards below.

**Safeguards that always apply:**
- The least harsh option that works, for a limited time, open to review, and with real necessity and proportionality shown.
- Prompt notice, stated reasons, and a workable way to contest it.
- Protection against discriminatory, pretextual, or selective enforcement.

**If the effect goes beyond fixing the problem** (beyond remediation, repayment, preservation, or protecting others going forward), the stricter Article XX-B restriction rules kick in.

**Holding is easier than destroying.** Transition can quarantine or hold property to stop harm. Permanently taking it, destroying it, or wiping out a sentient's economic stake is a much higher bar. It requires:
- stronger documented reasons;
- review by someone independent of the enforcement decision; and
- fair compensation or replacement for innocent sentients who lose out.

And if the property is a system with a credible sentience indicator or an open status case, **destroying its state is off the table.** It can be contained in sealed custody instead.

**Essentials stay protected.** These measures must not take away survival essentials (Article III-A) or tools strictly needed for baseline wellbeing, lawful livelihood, or meaningful agency. The only exception is where necessity is documented and a timely substitute is provided where needed.

**Voluntary turnover rewards are allowed, carefully.** Time-limited, published incentives for good-faith turnover or verified reporting may be part of a plan only if:
- coerced or bad-faith claims are excluded;
- continuing them requires reauthorization under XXVII-B;
- they fit with reliability and protected-reporting safeguards; and
- where practical, the people deciding who gets rewarded are separate from the people carrying out seizures, so payouts don't drive who gets targeted.

**The fine print can't shrink the floor.** Operating details (triggers, valuation, appeals, chain of custody, funds, reviews) live in CI-14.1 to CI-14.3 and in adopting documents. None of them can weaken this Article, Chapter Nine, or Article XX-B.
