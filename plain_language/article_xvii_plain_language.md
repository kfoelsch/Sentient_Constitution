# Article XVII, in Plain Language

## Innovation, Experimentation, and Creative Freedom

*Trying new things, and getting fairly rewarded for what you make.*

> **This is an everyday-reader version, not the official text.** If anything here seems to differ from the official [Article XVII](../core_06_rights_part_c.md#article-xvii-innovation-experimentation-and-creative-freedom), the official text is what counts.
>
> A note on "you" and "people": this constitution protects every sentient being it covers, not only humans. Here, "people" means all of them.

---

### The big idea

You are free to experiment, invent, and create.

When a project only affects you, and people who chose to take part, you get lighter rules. But calling something "just an experiment" doesn't get you out of anything. Once your project starts affecting other people, or other people start relying on it, the full rules apply.

You can also be rewarded for good ideas, but not by locking away things other people need to live, learn, fix things, or check the facts.

### Why this article exists

The article has two halves:

- **Freedom to try (Parts A–C):** when lighter rules apply, what you owe everyone else, and when your project has to move up to the full rules.
- **Fair reward (Parts D–F):** shared rules for rewarding inventors and creators (D), then specific rules for creative work (E) and for inventions (F).

It has two goals:

- **Let people thrive.** Give people room to try new things without heavy rules, as long as they're honest, others join by choice, and rewards leave room for everyone else to build, repair, and question.
- **Keep things safe over time.** Stop "it's only an experiment" from quietly becoming a permanent loophole, and stop useful inventions from turning into chokepoints that trap people.

---

### Part A: When the lighter rules apply

You have the right to experiment, build, and express yourself under lighter rules, but only if all three of these are true:

1. **It's actually contained.** Its effects stay inside the project.
2. **It can be undone.** Mistakes can be reversed.
3. **It isn't deeply connected to shared systems.** Other people's everyday systems don't depend on it.

Just calling something a "sandbox" isn't enough. It has to really be one.

> **Example:** A personal art generator you run on your own laptop qualifies. An "experimental" app that thousands of people now use to pay their bills does not.

---

### Part B: Be honest, and don't drag anyone in

**Tell people up front:**

- that it's an experiment, not a finished product;
- what the real risks are;
- where the walls of the sandbox are;
- what outside services, companies, or shared systems it depends on.

**Don't push your risks onto others.** If something could go wrong, it shouldn't spill onto bystanders or the systems everyone shares.

**Joining must be a real choice.** For riskier experiments, especially ones close to the basic systems people depend on:

- People should opt in on purpose wherever that's possible.
- Nobody should be signed up by a default setting, a pre-checked box, or a hidden connection.
- People should have a realistic way to undo things or recover. The bigger the risk, the stronger that safety net needs to be.

---

### Part C: Graduating out of the sandbox

Projects grow. When yours starts to have more impact, gets more people relying on it, becomes harder to undo, or gets more tied into shared systems, it has to move up to the full rules for real-world systems (see [Article XVI](../core_06_rights_part_c.md#article-xvi-system-lifecycle-environments-and-reversibility)).

That means:

- **Be open about it, and don't stall** to gain an advantage.
- **Add temporary safety measures** that match the current risk while you make the switch.
- **Stop calling it an experiment** for any part that's already having real-world effects.
- **Finish in a reasonable time.** The faster and bigger the growth, the faster the switch.

---

### Part D: Rewards for inventors and creators, without locking things up

**You can be rewarded** for ideas that are genuinely new, genuinely useful, and properly explained.

But rewards must be set up so that other people can still:

- build new things on top of your idea;
- get access to it;
- experiment with it;
- repair things that use it;
- make other products work with it;
- check whether your claims are true.

Rewards can't be designed to give you permanent control.

#### Exclusive rights must be small, short, and open to review

If you get the exclusive right to control an invention, design, method, or creative work, that right must be:

- **narrow:** it covers only what it needs to;
- **temporary:** it has an end date;
- **reviewable:** others can challenge it;
- **fair in size:** it matches what you actually contributed and what it really cost you to develop.

**It's up to you to justify** why you deserve exclusive rights. Nobody else has to prove you don't.

**Credit can last forever. Control can't.** Your name can stay on your work permanently, but exclusive control must end, and nobody may create fake scarcity.

**One clock for everything:** Creative works and inventions both get at most **30 years**, counted from when the work is made public. Parts E and F explain how each kind of work uses that time.

#### Creative work or invention?

What matters is what your claim actually controls, not what you call it.

- **Creative work (Part E):** how something is *expressed*: words, pictures, sound, music, story, characters, performance, or a purely decorative look.
- **Invention (Part F):** how something *works*: a method, process, machine, formula, interface, file format, protocol, or how a system behaves.

Lots of things are both, so each part is sorted separately:

| Thing | Creative part | Invention part |
|---|---|---|
| Software | The written code | What the code does |
| A game | Art, story, music | Code (rules and mechanics: see below) |
| A product design | A purely decorative look | A shape that affects how it works |
| Interfaces, file formats, protocols, AI models | — | All of it |

**Copyright can't be used to control how things work.** You can't use a creative-work claim (on a manual or on software, say) to stop people from repairing something, making things work together, or getting around the invention rules. If a claim would do that, that part is treated as an invention.

**Game rules and mechanics belong to everyone.** How a game is played (its rules and mechanics) can't be owned at all, as either a creative work or an invention. Anyone can use them. Only the art, story, music, and code that bring a game to life can be protected.

---

### Part E: Creative work

"Copyright-like protection" means a temporary right to control who copies, shares, displays, performs, adapts, or sells a creative work. When it ends, protections for credit, authenticity, and against fraud can continue.

Two terms decide when the clock starts:

- **Published** means you deliberately release the work to the public, to buyers, or to an open audience. Sharing privately, sending it for confidential review, working with collaborators, or storing it in an archive nobody can access is *not* publishing.
- **First appearance** means the first time a recognizable version is publicly available to anyone, including a free draft you posted online.

**The time limits:**

| Situation | Protection lasts up to |
|---|---|
| Published work | **30 years** after publication |
| Never published, but it has appeared publicly (e.g., a free draft posted online) | **50 years** after first appearance |
| Appeared first, then published later | **Whichever of those two limits ends first** |

> **Example:** You post a free draft in 2030, so the 50-year limit is 2080. You publish it in 2040, so the 30-year limit is 2070. Protection ends in **2070**, whichever comes first.

This is different from many current laws, which tie protection to the creator's lifetime plus several decades. Here, the clock depends on when the work came out, not when the creator dies.

**No tricks.** You can't use drafts or delayed publishing to:

- keep control forever;
- stop archives and libraries from preserving the work;
- block people from quoting or criticizing it;
- keep control over works that have become part of everyone's shared culture, education, safety standards, or information.

**Shorter protection, or none at all,** applies when a work is:

- paid for with public money;
- something people critically depend on;
- used like a standard;
- a basic part of education;
- relevant to safety;
- mainly used as shared culture or public information.

### Part F: Inventions

#### Show your work first

To get any reward, you must publicly explain your invention clearly enough that others can understand it, check it, and later rebuild it. Temporary secrecy is allowed only when it's truly justified. **An invention you keep secret earns no exclusive rights.** The clock starts on the day you make the explanation public.

#### Fifteen years exclusive, then fifteen years open for a fee

| Years after you go public | What you get |
|---|---|
| **0–15** | **Exclusive control.** You can say no to others using it. |
| **15–30** | **Open for a fee.** Anyone may use it, as long as they pay the published fee. You can't say no, stall, or add other conditions. |
| **After 30** | **Free for everyone.** Your name stays on it. |

These are the *longest* limits. Many inventions will get less (see below).

**How the fee is set: fair wages for time.** The fee is based on how much real good the invention does, measured in LEQU (Lifespan Equivalent Units). That's the constitution's standard way of measuring benefit: one LEQU is roughly one lifetime of wellbeing for one sentient being. That benefit is turned into money by treating it as *time* and paying a fair wage for it:

- If the invention saves people work, the fee is based on the working time it saves, paid at a fair wage.
- If it does other good, like saving lives or improving health, the fee is based on the lifetime-equivalent time LEQU measures, paid at a fair wage.
- The fee is only a *share* of the gain, so the people using the invention keep a real part of the benefit.

So the more an invention makes people more productive or better off, the more its inventor earns.

**How big a share?** Never more than half. The more widely an invention is used, the smaller the share per user, but never below 1%. It can never shrink so fast that the inventor earns less overall as more sentients use it. The default schedule:

| Sentients who benefit | Most the fee can take | Inventor's total reward (compared with 1,000 users) |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 1,000 | 50% of the gain | 1× |
| 10,000 | 25% | 5× |
| 1 million | about 6% | about 125× |
| 1 billion or more | 1% (the floor) | about 20,000× |

Widely used inventions earn far more in total, but each user pays a much smaller slice. The 1% floor applies only where there's a fee at all. For medicines, core standards, and other critical areas, a prize or public buyout may replace the fee entirely.

> **Example:** A new tool saves each of 10,000 workers one hour a week. The fee can be at most 25% of the value of that hour at a fair wage, so both the inventor and the users come out ahead.

The fee is *not* based on how badly someone needs it, how hard it is to switch, or what the market will bear. The fee schedule must be public, the same for everyone in similar situations, and open to challenge, and it can never be set so high that it blocks the uses listed below.

> **Example:** You publish the design for a new battery in 2030. Until 2045, only you, and whoever you choose, can make it. From 2045 to 2060, any company can make it by paying you the published fee. From 2060, anyone can make it for free.

#### Some uses can never be blocked

In either phase, invention rights can't be used, beyond what's strictly necessary and open to review, to block:

- repairs;
- safety work;
- making things work together;
- archiving;
- research;
- education;
- switching to a different provider.

#### The more people depend on it, the tighter the limits

The constitution sorts systems by how much the world depends on them. For the most important ones (survival-critical systems, critical systems many people depend on, and widely coordinated systems):

- Rewards that **keep access open** are strongly preferred.
- Exclusive rights must be **especially narrow**, **quickly reviewable**, and **easy to override** when keeping things running, compatibility, repair, or the public interest is at stake.
- Either phase can be shortened, or an invention can go **straight to "open for a fee"** with no exclusive phase at all.

Smaller, low-stakes inventions can use the full 15 + 15 years, as long as the details are really disclosed, people can easily switch to something else, and protections against lock-in actually work.

#### Areas where locking things up is presumed off-limits

Strong exclusive rights are presumed a bad idea, and may be banned outright, for:

- medicines and public-health essentials;
- infrastructure people need to survive;
- core communication and compatibility standards;
- foundational scientific knowledge;
- the tools this constitution uses for safety, auditing, and compliance checks.

In these areas, and other survival-critical or standard-setting ones, the preferred approach is to reward inventors with **prizes**, **direct payment**, **shared pools**, **required licensing**, or **the public buying the rights**, so everyone can still use, repair, and spread the invention.

#### When a small thing becomes a big thing

Sometimes something that started small becomes a system everyone runs through, like a platform, a protocol, an AI model, an online marketplace, or a payment network. When that happens, it must be re-evaluated. Any remaining exclusive rights can be **narrowed**, **converted** (for example, into required licensing), or **ended** if they would:

- trap people with no real way out;
- choke off competition;
- seriously threaten the stability of shared systems, access to the truth, or fair participation.

Rules on publishing, reviewing, repeating, and correcting scientific work used to be part of this article. They now live in [Article XIV-D](../core_06_rights_part_c.md#article-xiv-d-scientific-publication-review-and-replication-integrity), under Info-Sphere Integrity, alongside the other rules on keeping shared information honest.

### The whole article in one breath

Experiment freely when it only affects you and willing participants. Be honest about the risks. Step up to the full rules as soon as your project matters to other people. Get rewarded for good ideas, but only for a limited time, and never by locking up what others need.
