# Article XIX, in Plain Language

## Interoperability, Portability, Movement, Refuge, and Exit Integrity

*Leaving, switching, moving, and never being left without protection.*

> **This is an everyday-reader version, not the official text.** If anything here seems to differ from the official [Article XIX](../core_06_rights_part_c.md#article-xix-interoperability-portability-movement-refuge-and-exit-integrity), the official text is what counts.
>
> A note on "you" and "people": this constitution protects every sentient being it covers, not only humans. Here, "people" means all of them.

---

### The big idea

**You can always leave.** You can leave a service, a platform, or a system that no longer works for you. You can move to another place. And if the place you live seriously breaks this constitution's rules, you can seek refuge somewhere that follows them.

Leaving has to work **in real life**, not just on paper. That means:

- you can take your data and your identity with you;
- the systems you depend on can't suddenly cut you off without warning;
- nobody can build traps that make leaving too costly, confusing, or slow;
- you are never left with **no one at all** responsible for protecting your basic rights.

### Why this article exists

The article has four parts:

- **Part A:** taking your data and identity with you.
- **Part B:** systems that connect to each other must play fair.
- **Part C:** no traps designed to keep you from leaving.
- **Part D:** moving, seeking refuge, and never being left without protection.

It has two goals:

- **Let people thrive.** People can choose, switch, and move between systems and places, so no one is stuck with a service or a government that doesn't serve them.
- **Keep things safe over time.** As people come to rely more on a system, or as companies change hands and governments change, the ability to leave stays real. When things fall apart, nobody falls through the cracks.

---

### Part A: Take your stuff with you

When a system holds your data or your identity, or depends on it, you must be able to **take it with you** in a form you can actually use somewhere else.

"Your stuff" includes:

- **your data:** files, photos, messages, records, contacts;
- **your identity:** your accounts, names, and credentials;
- **your working state:** your settings and history, and for digital beings, the state they need to keep running as themselves.

The system must:

- **explain** how to move it;
- **hand it over quickly enough** that you can really leave, switch, or replace the service.

It may hold things back only for real, proportionate safety or security reasons. It can't block you with:

- formats nobody else can read;
- deliberately lower-quality copies;
- punishing terms for people who leave.

> **Example:** You decide to switch photo services. You're entitled to all your photos, at full quality, in a standard format, with their dates and albums, in time to actually make the switch. You don't get a blurry, scrambled download that arrives months later.

---

### Part B: Systems that connect must play fair

When a system connects with other systems, and people or other systems depend on that connection:

- **Publish the rules.** It must document how others can connect to it, and those terms must work both ways. The more others depend on it, the clearer and fairer those terms must be.
- **Warn before narrowing.** If it's going to cut back access or change how others connect, it must give notice **early enough** for them to adjust, move elsewhere, or challenge the change.
- **Justify any narrowing.** Cutting back is allowed only when there's a real reason that can be checked.

> **Example:** A platform that thousands of small shops rely on can't shut off the tool they use to manage orders with two days' notice. It has to announce the change in time for them to adapt or move, and be able to explain why.

---

### Part C: No traps

Anything whose **main effect** is to stop you from leaving, switching, replacing a service, or challenging it breaks this article. That includes:

- file formats nobody else can read;
- deliberately making things incompatible without a good reason;
- harsh penalties or pressure tactics for switching;
- hiding information you need to make the move.

Ordinary, reasonable switching costs are fine. Barriers built to trap you are not, whatever they're called.

> **Example:** An app that lets you in easily but hides the "export" option, charges a steep fee to leave, and deletes your history if you try is a trap. The app might call these features. This article treats them as violations.

---

### Part D: Moving, refuge, and never being left without protection

#### You can move

You have the right to move within and between places and systems of government. That includes relocating when staying would seriously harm your:

- survival;
- dignity;
- basic rights;
- freedom from manipulation.

For people with bodies, that means physically moving. For digital and hybrid beings, it means the equivalent, such as moving to a different host or hardware, within real safety limits.

#### You can seek refuge

If the place you live **seriously breaks this constitution's rules**, you can seek refuge in a place that follows them.

- The place you go to must **consider your request**, and grant it where that fits its own basic-rights duties.
- It can't turn you away because of what kind of body or hardware you run on.
- It **can** refuse or set conditions for people who are actively hostile to the constitution or have serious unrepaired attacks on it on their record, but only when that's necessary, fits the situation, and follows a fair process.

**Forced mass expulsions are the expelling government's fault.** A government that deliberately pushes large numbers of people out to overwhelm its neighbors is breaking the constitution. That doesn't make any one neighbor automatically responsible for taking everyone in, as long as someone still recognizes and protects the people involved.

**Climate refuge is left to each community to decide.** This article doesn't say whether a place becoming unlivable because of climate is, by itself, a reason to grant refuge. Communities that decide must do it publicly, in a way people can challenge. That choice can't weaken any other movement or refuge right.

#### You can never be left with no one

No one may be left without a government or system that will:

- recognize their basic rights;
- decide their standing fairly;
- give them a way to get harm fixed.

How that works:

- **The place you came from stays responsible first.** If it still exists and can recognize you, that's its job.
- **Backup kicks in if it can't or won't.** If it has collapsed, refuses, or disappears, shared or regional backup arrangements must make sure you never reach zero.
- **This isn't a duty for any one place to take in everyone.** It's a guarantee that someone, somewhere, is responsible for you.

When a system collapses, a community leaves the arrangement, or governments change, your protections under this constitution **don't disappear**. Transition arrangements must carry them over.

People who are openly hostile to the constitution may face conditions, monitoring, or restricted status, but they keep their basic rights, their access to remedies, and fair process.

#### Leaving and moving work together

Sometimes one move involves both kinds of rights. For example, a digital being might move between regions by moving to a new host. When that happens, **both** sets of protections apply in full, and neither one shrinks the other.

#### Limits on restricting movement must be fair

Any limit on moving, migrating, or seeking refuge must be:

- truly **necessary**;
- **proportionate** to the problem;
- **narrow**, and the least restrictive option that works.

It can never be based on race, sex, disability, or other protected traits, or on stand-ins for them. And it can't treat people as a demographic group instead of looking at each person's own situation.

#### Real dangers go through real justice

This article doesn't protect anyone from lawful detention or supervision when they've been **proven** to be violent, coercive, or seriously dangerous. But those measures must:

- go through a full, fair justice process;
- use the least restrictive measure, for the shortest time needed;
- never leave the person without some authority that protects their basic rights and lets them seek a remedy, including while they're in custody.

#### Standing isn't a border

Your **standing** decides which roles and privileges you can use ([Article XVIII](../core_06_rights_part_c.md#article-xviii-standing-and-participation-status)). Lower standing, or a lock on some role, can **never by itself**:

- stop you from moving or migrating;
- deny you refuge;
- stop you from taking your data and records with you;
- trap you in a system;
- leave you without any country or authority that protects you.

Standing is not a stand-in for a fair trial.

---

### The whole article in one breath

You can take your things and leave. Systems you depend on must play fair and warn you before changing the rules. Traps built to keep you from leaving are violations. You can move, and seek refuge from places that break this constitution. And no matter what collapses or who turns you away, someone must always be responsible for protecting your basic rights.
